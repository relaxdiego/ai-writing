I'd write the events into the application Postgres and ship them from there to an append-only archive, keeping a rolling window locally. The team is split because two separate questions have been collapsed into one: where a new event lands at write time, and where seven years of history lives. Those have genuinely different answers, and picking one store for both is what makes the choice feel like a trade-off.

The case for landing in Postgres is atomicity. If the audit row is written in the same transaction as the mutation it describes, you cannot lose an event to a crash between the commit and the ship. Send events directly to an external store instead and you have introduced a window in which the business fact is durable and the audit record is not, which is exactly the gap an auditor will ask about. Recovering from it means building an outbox anyway, so you may as well build the outbox deliberately and let a shipper drain it.

The case against keeping everything in Postgres is operational rather than about throughput. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second on average, maybe a few hundred at peak, which Postgres will not notice. Seven years of it is roughly five billion rows, and at 500 bytes to a kilobyte of payload that is several terabytes before indexes and probably six to eight after. That volume changes the character of your application database: base backups and restores get slower, major-version upgrades need a much longer window or a logical replication dance, and the audit table dominates your disk and your page cache while contributing nothing to serving traffic. The failure mode is not that queries get slow, it's that a production restore during an incident now carries five billion rows of history you don't need to be back online.

The join requirement is weaker than it looks, and I'd examine it before letting it drive the decision. An audit event that resolves the actor's name or email by joining to the current user row will tell a support investigator what that user is called today, not what they were called when they did the thing. For an event from 2021 that is often actively misleading, and for a user who has since been deleted the join returns nothing at all. Audit records should carry a denormalized snapshot of whatever identity and context mattered at write time: actor id, display name, email, role, tenant, IP. Once they do, the live join drops from a requirement to an occasional convenience, and the convenience is almost always wanted for recent events rather than for something from four years ago.

Compliance usually wants tamper-evidence, not just retention, and a table in your primary database is difficult to argue as append-only. You can revoke UPDATE and DELETE from application roles and add triggers that reject them, and that is worth doing, but a migration or a superuser session still bypasses it, and the person auditing you knows that. An object store with a WORM retention lock, or an equivalent immutable store, gives you a control you can point at rather than a policy you have to promise. Hash-chaining each batch as you write it out costs almost nothing and lets you demonstrate that the archive hasn't been rewritten.

One tension to resolve early: an immutable seven-year log collides with erasure requests. The usual answer is to keep personal data out of the immutable payload, storing a pseudonymous subject id and holding the mapping to identity in a normal, deletable table, or to encrypt per-subject fields with a key you can destroy. Decide this before you have four billion rows, because retrofitting it means rewriting an archive you have just promised nobody can rewrite.

Concretely, that shape is a partitioned audit table in Postgres, monthly partitions, BRIN index on the timestamp and a btree on actor and object id, with a shipper that reads sealed partitions into compressed Parquet in object storage under a retention lock and then drops the local partition. Keep something like ninety days hot, which is where essentially every support investigation lands and where the ad-hoc join to user records is genuinely useful. Older material stays queryable through DuckDB or Athena over the archive, at a latency that is fine for the handful of times a year someone needs 2022. Compressed columnar storage will hold the full seven years in a few hundred gigabytes, at a cost per terabyte-month that makes the retention requirement a rounding error rather than a capacity plan.
