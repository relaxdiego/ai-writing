I did not find the cause, so what follows is where the investigation stopped rather than a plausible story. The failures are not random: in the last eight runs the short ones fall on Aug 23, 26 and 29, every third day, at roughly 22–24k rows against 48k. Runtime tracks the row count, about seven minutes against about fifteen. The proportionality is the most useful signal I have, because a job that dropped rows late — a partial write, a transaction rolling back part of the way through `jobs/nightly_rollup.py:23` — would still spend the full time fetching and grouping before losing anything. These runs don't. Less input reached the loop, which moves the question upstream to `fetch_events` and the window it is handed.

That window is computed at `jobs/scheduler.py:61` as `last_success_at or (now - 24h)`, and it is the obvious way a window shrinks: if anything advances `last_success_at` between nightly runs, whether a manual backfill, a second scheduler replica, or the retry path added in 9d02e1e, the next run starts from that later mark and covers only a fraction of the day. I could not confirm any of that. `job_runs` does not record the window bounds, so I have no direct evidence of how wide the window actually was on the short days, and I did not trace where `last_success_at` is written or whether it is scoped per job. I also only read the subject line of the retry commit, not its diff. The three-day spacing corresponds to nothing I looked at; it is not a weekday pattern.

The explanation I cannot rule out is that the job is behaving correctly and the source simply holds half as many events on those days, from an upstream producer running on its own cycle. Seen from inside the rollup the two causes are indistinguishable: both give you half the rows in half the time and an `ok` status.

Separating them takes two observations. Log `window_start` and `window_end` on every run, and count events in the source table by hour for Aug 29 against Aug 30. A full day of source events on Aug 29 under a twelve-hour window points at `last_success_at`; a source that is itself half empty puts the defect upstream, with the rollup reporting it faithfully.
