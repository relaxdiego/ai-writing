**Separate store — but "separate" should mean a dedicated Postgres instance with a cold tier, not a new class of technology.**

Here's the reasoning.

**The write volume is a non-issue; the accumulation is the whole problem.** 2M events/day is ~23 writes/second average. Postgres will not notice. But 2M × 365 × 7 ≈ 5.1 billion rows. At a plausible 400–800 bytes per event with a JSON payload, that's 2–4 TB before indexes, probably 4–6 TB with them. The question isn't "can Postgres hold this" (it can, with partitioning) — it's what that mass does to every operation you perform on the database that *also* serves your application.

That's the argument that actually decides it. A 5 TB app database means your base backups take hours, your restore-time objective gets measured in a workday, your major-version upgrades become projects, your replica rebuilds are painful, and your `pg_dump`-a-staging-copy workflow dies. And you're paying all of that for data that has almost nothing to do with serving requests. Audit logs have a completely different access pattern (write-once, read approximately never, scan-not-seek when read) and a completely different lifecycle (7 years, immutable) from application data (read-heavy, mutable, deleted when the user leaves). Coupling them means the app data's operational needs are held hostage by the audit data's volume, and the audit data's retention is held hostage by the app data's migration schedule.

**The compliance argument is real but secondary, and weaker than people usually claim.** Yes, in the same database your application's role can `UPDATE` and `DELETE` audit rows. You can mitigate that a lot — separate role, revoke UPDATE/DELETE, `BEFORE UPDATE` trigger that raises, append-only by convention. But any migration, any superuser, any `psql` session from an on-call engineer can still rewrite history, and "we revoked a grant" is a much weaker story to an auditor than "it's in a different system with different credentials, and the cold tier is in object storage with a retention lock." If your compliance regime has teeth, physical separation is the cheaper conversation.

**The join requirement is the weakest argument for co-location, and I'd push back on the premise.** Joining audit events to *current* user records is arguably a correctness bug, not a feature. If a user changes their email, gets renamed, or is deleted under GDPR, your audit record's meaning silently changes underneath you — the log now says something different about the past than it did last year. Audit events should be self-contained snapshots: denormalize `actor_id`, `actor_email_at_time`, `actor_role_at_time`, `tenant_id` into the event at write time. That's what you actually want for an investigation ("what did this account look like when it did this?"), and it removes the join from the hot path entirely.

For the residual cases where you genuinely need live user context, support investigations are low-volume and latency-tolerant. Any of these will do: query both and join in the application; `postgres_fdw` from the audit DB to a read replica; or replicate a slim user dimension table (id, email, org, status) into the audit DB nightly. None of these is worth 4 TB in your primary.

**The shape I'd build:**

- **Hot tier:** dedicated Postgres, one table partitioned by month, ~12–18 months retained. That's ~700M–1B rows, which is comfortable with partition pruning and a handful of well-chosen indexes (actor_id, tenant_id, occurred_at, and a GIN on the payload only if you actually need it).
- **Cold tier:** monthly partitions aged out to Parquet in object storage with a WORM/retention lock, queryable ad hoc via DuckDB or Athena. Storage cost for years 2–7 drops by roughly an order of magnitude, and the immutability story becomes trivially defensible.
- **Write path:** async, buffered, and *never* in the request's transaction with app data — a failed audit write should not fail a user action, and vice versa. Accept the small durability gap or use an outbox table in the app DB drained by a worker if you need exactly-once.

**What would change my answer:** if volume were 20k/day and retention 1 year, keep it in the app database and don't build any of this — the operational overhead of a second system would dominate. If a regulator has specified a tamper-evidence standard, you may need hash-chaining or a genuine ledger product rather than "Postgres plus S3 object lock." And if your team has no capacity to run a second database, a managed Postgres instance is still the right shape — just don't let "we're split on this" turn into "we'll deal with it later," because retrofitting a 4 TB table out of a production primary is dramatically worse than starting it separate.
