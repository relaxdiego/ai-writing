I'd keep it in Postgres, but on its own instance rather than sharing the application database — and I'd invest the saved effort in partitioning, actor denormalization, and an archive tier. Here's the reasoning, including the part where the join requirement turns out to matter less than it first appears.

**Volume isn't the constraint.** Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes per second on average, maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for Postgres. Over seven years you land at roughly 5.1 billion rows, and at a typical 500–900 bytes per event that's somewhere in the 3–5 TB range before indexes. With declarative monthly partitioning you get about eighty-four partitions of ~60M rows each, which stay individually indexable, vacuum in reasonable time, and can be detached without a rewrite. Nothing about the numbers pushes you toward a specialized store on capability grounds, so the decision has to be made on operational coupling instead.

**The join requirement should be designed away, not designed around.** An audit event needs to record who the actor was *at the time of the event*, not who they are now. If a support investigation surfaces a user's current email and role rather than the values that were in effect when the action happened, your audit trail is quietly wrong in exactly the situations where it matters most — after a role change, after a rename, after an offboarding. So you want to denormalize actor identity, tenant context, and any authorization state into the event row regardless of where the events live. Once you've done that, the remaining cross-references are rare, latency-tolerant hydrations by primary key rather than genuine relational joins, and they're perfectly well served by an application-layer lookup or a `postgres_fdw` foreign table for the ad-hoc case.

**What actually decides it is backup and restore coupling.** Postgres physical backups, WAL archiving, and point-in-time recovery all operate at the cluster level, so putting audit in a separate *database* on the same cluster buys you namespace hygiene and no isolation whatsoever. If your application database is currently 200 GB and you bolt 4 TB of audit data onto the same cluster, you've made every base backup and every restore drill twenty times slower, and you've tied your application's recovery time objective to data that has no bearing on whether the product is up. The rule of thumb I'd use: if projected audit volume exceeds your application data by more than roughly two or three times, it belongs on its own instance. At 3–5 TB, that's true for most teams.

**Append-only is a permissions and integrity property, not a product category.** You get most of what a dedicated append-only store sells by creating a writer role with `INSERT` granted and `UPDATE`/`DELETE` revoked, keeping DDL rights away from the application, and — if your auditors want tamper-evidence rather than just tamper-resistance — chaining each row's hash to its predecessor so that a silent deletion or edit is detectable. Before anyone argues this further, it's worth ten minutes to read what your compliance regime actually requires: some name WORM or immutable storage explicitly, in which case the debate is over and you ship to object storage with an object-lock policy. Most say "protected from unauthorized modification," which the Postgres approach satisfies.

**Seven years belongs in cold storage, not hot tables.** Keep something like ninety to a hundred and eighty days of partitions attached and indexed for interactive investigation, then detach older partitions, write them to compressed Parquet in object storage under a lifecycle policy that runs to the seven-year mark, and query them with DuckDB or Athena on the rare occasions someone needs 2021 data. Storage cost drops by an order of magnitude, the live database stays small enough to reason about, and a detached partition can be re-attached if an investigation needs it back in SQL.

Three things would change my answer. If your compliance regime names immutable or WORM storage specifically, go to object storage with object-lock and stop optimizing for the join. If the events are payload-heavy — full request and response bodies rather than structured facts — the volume estimate could be ten times higher and Postgres stops being comfortable. And if you already operate a log pipeline into ClickHouse or similar, use it: the marginal operational cost is near zero, and ClickHouse is genuinely better than Postgres at this shape of data. The recommendation above is for the case where standing up a second class of datastore is a real new burden on the team, which is the situation most teams asking this question are actually in.
