I'd keep the audit log in the same Postgres database as the application data, in its own schema, and handle the seven-year retention with partitioning plus an archive tier rather than with a second system. The volume argument doesn't favor separation: 2 million events a day is about 23 writes per second on average and maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for Postgres. The number that actually matters is the total, roughly 5 billion rows over seven years, or single-digit terabytes once you account for payloads and indexes, and that is a storage and operations problem rather than a throughput one.

Two properties push toward colocation. The first is that an audit record written inside the same transaction as the action it describes commits if and only if the action commits. Ship events to a separate store and you need an outbox table, a relay process, and a story for what happens when the relay is down or double-delivers; you have replaced a database guarantee with a distributed systems problem, and for a log whose purpose is establishing what happened, that gap is exactly the wrong place to introduce ambiguity. The second is the support investigations you mentioned. Postgres cannot join across databases without a foreign data wrapper, so if you want `audit.events` joined to `public.users` in a plain query, it has to be the same database and not merely the same cluster.

The honest cost of colocation is operational coupling, and it's worth taking seriously. Seven years of audit data inflates your base backups, lengthens restore time when you're recovering the application under pressure, and adds a large table set to every vacuum and failover consideration. The fix is to not keep seven years online: declarative monthly partitions on the event timestamp, an online window of twelve to eighteen months, and a job that detaches older partitions, exports them to object storage as Parquet, and drops them. Support investigations almost always concern recent activity, so the hot window covers the join use case, and the rare older query goes to Athena or DuckDB over the archive. The archive tier also improves your compliance posture rather than weakening it, since S3 Object Lock in compliance mode is genuine write-once storage, which is stronger than anything a relational database can offer you.

For the online window, append-only in Postgres means a dedicated role with `INSERT` granted and `UPDATE` and `DELETE` revoked, no application path holding stronger rights, and the table owned by a separate role the app doesn't authenticate as. This stops the application and ordinary operators, and it does not stop a superuser or anyone with filesystem access to the data directory. Most regimes accept role-based controls plus immutable backups as sufficient, so check whether yours requires tamper-evidence beyond that before assuming the gap is fatal.

Where I'd go the other way: if your auditors demand cryptographic tamper-evidence such as hash chaining or a notarized ledger across the full retention period, if the audit stream feeds a SIEM that other systems also write into, or if your application database is sharded per tenant, in which case in-band audit writes fragment the log across shards and the join you want stops being local anyway.
