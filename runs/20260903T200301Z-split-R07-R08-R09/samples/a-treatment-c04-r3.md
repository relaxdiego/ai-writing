Write audit events into the application Postgres, in the same transaction as the action that produced them, but keep only a bounded hot window there and age everything older into a separate append-only archive. The argument the team is having assumes these are alternatives, and the two requirements that actually bind, transactional completeness and seven-year retention, pull in opposite directions on a timescale that lets you satisfy both in sequence.

The reason not to ship straight to a separate store is the write path. If the audit record lands somewhere your business transaction cannot commit or roll back with it, you have a log that can miss events for actions that succeeded, or contain events for actions that rolled back. For a compliance log that is the worst available failure, and the usual fixes are worse than the problem: dual writes lose records at exactly the moments you most want them, and a transactional outbox means you are writing to Postgres first anyway, so you may as well write the real record there rather than a copy of it. Throughput is not the constraint here. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second on average, a few hundred at peak, which is nothing to a Postgres instance already carrying your application load.

The reason not to keep everything there is size over time. Seven years at that rate is roughly 5.1 billion rows, likely somewhere between two and five terabytes before indexes. Query performance is manageable with partitioning; the thing that degrades quietly is your recovery. Physical backups are cluster-wide, so a multi-terabyte audit table sits inside every base backup and inside your restore time for an application outage that has nothing to do with audit. Your RTO ends up governed by data you would never need in the first hour of an incident.

So bound the window. Partition the audit table by month, declaratively, and keep something like the last 90 days to 12 months resident. At 90 days that is 180 million rows, on the order of a hundred gigabytes, which does not distort backups. Monthly partitions also make both ends of the retention policy cheap: aging a partition out is a detach and export rather than a `DELETE` against billions of rows, and expiry at seven years is a `DROP TABLE` instead of a multi-day vacuum problem. Build the legal-hold path before you need it, since "delete at seven years except these" is the requirement that breaks naive expiry jobs.

For the archive, columnar files in object storage with Object Lock, queried through DuckDB or Athena, is the low-operational-cost choice; ClickHouse is the right answer instead if support staff query the archive often enough that interactive latency matters. Audit events compress extremely well, so expect the cold tier to cost tens of dollars a month rather than the hundreds or thousands the same data costs on provisioned SSD.

On the join, which is what the team is really arguing about: it is a weaker constraint than it looks, and you should weaken it further deliberately. Denormalize the actor's identity into the event at write time, not just `user_id` but the email, display name, and role as they were at that moment. You want this regardless of storage, because an audit record that resolves to today's user row is misleading evidence: it will tell an investigator the actor was an admin when the action was taken by someone who has since been promoted. Once identity is embedded, the common support query is a filter on a column rather than a join, and it works identically in the hot table and the cold archive. Keep the foreign key for the recent window where a live join genuinely helps, and accept that investigations reaching back years are rare, allowed to be slower, and better served by the frozen copy of the facts.

One caution about append-only within Postgres. Revoking `UPDATE` and `DELETE` from the application role and adding a trigger that raises on either gets you real protection against application bugs and ordinary compromise, but not against anyone with superuser. If your auditors want tamper evidence rather than tamper resistance, that comes from hash-chaining each event to its predecessor and periodically anchoring the chain head somewhere outside the database. That works the same whether the rows live in Postgres or elsewhere, so it is not a reason to choose the separate store.
