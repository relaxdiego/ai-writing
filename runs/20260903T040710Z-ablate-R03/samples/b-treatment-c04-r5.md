I'd write the audit events into Postgres and ship them out of it, rather than choosing one or the other. The framing as a binary is what's splitting the team, and both camps are right about the thing they're worried about: the people defending Postgres want the transactional guarantee and the joins, and the people defending a separate store want tamper evidence and a database that doesn't grow to five terabytes.

Start with the number that isn't the problem. Two million events a day is about 23 writes a second averaged, maybe a few hundred at peak. Postgres will not notice that, so throughput shouldn't appear in the argument at all. The number that does matter is 5.1 billion rows over seven years, which at a realistic 500 bytes to a kilobyte of JSON per event lands somewhere between 2.5 and 5 TB before indexes. Postgres will hold that if you partition by month, but you pay for it in places that have nothing to do with query speed: your base backups and your restore time now belong to the audit log rather than to the application, and your recovery objective for a customer-facing outage is set by data nobody needs in order to bring the service back up. That coupling, not raw size, is the strongest operational reason to get old audit data out.

The reason to keep the write path in Postgres is atomicity. If the audit record is written in the same transaction as the business change, an event cannot exist for a change that rolled back, and a change cannot commit without its event. Dual-writing to an external store from application code gives that up immediately, and you will discover the gap during the one investigation where it matters. So the audit table lives in the application database, ideally in its own schema on its own tablespace, written by a role that has INSERT and nothing else, with UPDATE and DELETE revoked and a trigger that raises on both. That gets you most of what the append-only camp wants inside the database they distrust, and it makes the remaining exposure explicit: a superuser or a compromised migration can still rewrite history, which is precisely what an external copy is for.

The archive is where the compliance requirement actually gets satisfied. Export sealed partitions to object storage as Parquet on a lag of a month or so, verify the export, then detach and drop the partition. With S3 Object Lock in compliance mode, nobody including your own root account can alter or delete those objects before the retention date, which is a far cleaner story to tell an auditor than "we revoked DELETE from the application role." Columnar compression on structured audit events routinely gets an order of magnitude, so the seven-year tail is likely a few hundred gigabytes rather than five terabytes, and at Glacier Instant Retrieval prices it costs tens of dollars a month against roughly a thousand for the equivalent sitting in multi-AZ RDS with backups. Queries against it go through Athena or DuckDB and take seconds instead of milliseconds, which is the right trade for data whose access pattern is a legal or forensic request rather than a support ticket.

That leaves the joins, and I'd push back gently on how much weight they're carrying. Support investigations are overwhelmingly about recent activity, so a hot window of twelve to eighteen months in Postgres covers them with an ordinary join and no new tooling. More importantly, joining a 2031 query against a 2026 audit event gives you the user's name and email as they are today, not as they were when the event happened, which is usually not the answer the investigation wanted. Denormalise the actor's identity and relevant attributes into the event at write time. Once you do, the live join reduces to filtering by user id, and the archive stops needing a join at all.

Two things to settle before you build it. Retention of seven years and a right-to-erasure request are in direct tension, and the usual resolution is to keep personal data out of the audit payload beyond stable identifiers, or to encrypt per-subject fields with a key you can destroy. And events written next month must be readable in 2033 by people who no longer work there, so version every record and treat the payload as semi-structured from the start rather than migrating a schema across five billion rows later.
