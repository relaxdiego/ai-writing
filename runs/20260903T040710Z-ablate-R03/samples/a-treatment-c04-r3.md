Separate store, and I'd make it a separate Postgres rather than a different technology. Worth clearing one argument out of the way first, because it's usually the one teams fight about: throughput is not the issue here. Two million events a day is roughly 23 writes a second averaged out, a few hundred at peak, which any Postgres primary absorbs without noticing. What distinguishes the two options is accumulation and lifecycle.

Seven years at that rate is about five billion rows. Depending on whether your events carry a JSON diff payload or just a handful of identifiers, that's somewhere between one and several terabytes of heap before indexes, and it's worth measuring your actual average row size early because it swings the numbers a lot. Postgres can hold it. The cost is that every operational property of your application database ends up dictated by data you almost never read: backup windows, restore time during an incident, major-version upgrade planning, replica rebuild time, autovacuum behaviour on what is now by far the largest table in the cluster. A 200 GB application database can be restored inside an incident call. A 5 TB one, 96% of which is audit history from 2021, cannot, and that history is the part you'd least mind being unavailable for an hour.

Retention itself is not the differentiator people expect, because you'd partition by month in either design, and dropping an 84th partition is cheap either way. The sharper difference is the trust boundary. Compliance regimes generally want the audit log to function as evidence, which means the credentials that can append to it should not be able to rewrite it. Co-locating gets you partway there with a dedicated role and revoked UPDATE and DELETE, but superuser and your backup tooling still span both, and there's a failure mode that co-location cannot fix: restoring application data to a point in time silently rewinds the audit log along with it, which is precisely backwards for a record whose job is to survive whatever happened to the application.

The join is the real cost of separating, and it's smaller than it looks. Support investigations are human-paced and low-QPS, so you have several cheap options: postgres_fdw against the audit instance, or replicating the user dimension into the audit store on a nightly schedule. Better still, denormalise at write time and capture the actor's email, display name and role as of the event. For audit purposes those point-in-time attributes are the correct semantics anyway, since users get renamed, reassigned and deleted, and a record saying "user 4417 performed X" is materially worse evidence than one saying who that person was and what authority they held at the moment they acted. A good portion of the join you're worried about losing is a join that shouldn't exist.

On the write path, don't let "separate store" become fire-and-forget over the network, because that trades a coupling problem for a durability problem. Write the event to an outbox table inside the same transaction as the business action, then ship asynchronously to the audit store. You keep atomicity with the thing being audited while keeping the two databases' lifecycles independent, and the outbox stays small because rows leave it as soon as they're shipped. If you want to compress the footprint further later, keep a hot window of ninety days queryable (about 180 million rows, entirely comfortable) and roll older partitions out to compressed columnar files on object storage with a catalog over them, since most seven-year obligations are "produce on request" rather than "query interactively."

The case for going the other way is operational capacity, not architecture. If your team has no room to run and monitor a second database, then a single cluster with monthly partitioning, a dedicated append-only role, and an explicit plan to split is a defensible choice, and the split stays tractable because partitions can be moved independently. What I'd avoid is landing there by default, since the coupling you accept is quiet for about two years and then expensive all at once.
