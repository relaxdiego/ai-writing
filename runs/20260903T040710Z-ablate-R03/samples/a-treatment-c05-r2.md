I did not find the cause. Here is what the evidence does and does not support, and where I would look next.

The failures are not partial writes. Short nights and full nights both report status `ok`, and the write loop is wrapped in a single transaction, so a run that died mid-write would roll back rather than leave half the rows. What separates the two kinds of night is duration: the ~48k-row runs take about fifteen minutes, the ~23k-row runs take about seven. The job is not producing less output from the same input, it is doing about half as much work end to end. That points upstream of `write_rollup`, at `fetch_events` and the window it is given, rather than at the summarise or write path.

The obvious suspect is `window_start = last_success_at or (now - timedelta(hours=24))` in `jobs/scheduler.py:61`. If `last_success_at` is ever recorded as something other than the previous window's end, or is stored in a different timezone or with a different clock than `now`, the job would silently roll up a short window and still report success, which is exactly the shape of what we are seeing. I did not verify this. I never read how `last_success_at` is written, and I never read `fetch_events`, so this remains a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism and no confirmation behind it.

One thing in the data argues against that story, or at least complicates it. In the eight runs I pulled, the short nights are 23, 26 and 29 August: an exact three-day spacing. A drifting or misrecorded watermark would not naturally produce that regularity, whereas an upstream export, a partition rotation or a cache with a three-day cadence would. Eight rows is too few to call this a pattern rather than a coincidence, and the `job_runs` table holds thirty; reading the remaining twenty-two is the cheapest way to tell those two explanations apart, since the watermark theory survives an irregular schedule and dies against a strict one.

Two other gaps are worth naming. I did not check whether a short night covers fewer accounts or the same accounts with fewer events each, which distinguishes a truncated time window from a filtered or partially available source. And I did not date the two commits under `jobs/` against the onset of the problem; `9d02e1e`, which added retries around transient fetch errors, is the kind of change that can turn a partial upstream failure into a quiet short read.
