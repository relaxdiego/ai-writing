I'd keep the audit log in Postgres and age it out to object storage, rather than standing up a second live datastore for it.

The decisive argument is the write path. If audit events go to a separate system, every business operation has to write to two places, and the moment one write succeeds and the other fails you have either a missing audit record or an audit record for something that didn't happen. Working around that means an outbox table in Postgres plus a shipper, at which point you're running Postgres as the source of truth anyway and paying for a second system on top. Writing the audit row in the same transaction as the change it describes gives you the property compliance actually cares about: the record exists if and only if the event did.

The volume doesn't argue against this. Two million events a day is about twenty-five writes per second on average, which is nothing; the pressure is storage, not throughput. Seven years is roughly 5.1 billion rows, somewhere between 1.5 and 5 TB with indexes depending on how fat your payloads are, and that mass is what makes people nervous. It shouldn't sit in your operational database, because it inflates every base backup, every restore drill, and every replica rebuild for data nobody queries. So partition by month from day one, keep a hot window in Postgres, and detach older partitions on a schedule. I'd size the hot window from your actual support lookback rather than guessing: pull the age distribution of the last few hundred investigations, and if the ninety-fifth percentile is under a year, keep eighteen months hot and tier the rest. Eighteen months is about a billion rows across eighteen partitions, which Postgres handles comfortably as long as queries carry a timestamp predicate.

Detached partitions go to S3 as Parquet under Object Lock in compliance mode, which is the genuinely append-only, genuinely immutable part of the design, and it's cheap. Query them with Athena or DuckDB when an investigation reaches back that far. The one requirement this puts on the schema is that audit rows must be self-contained: snapshot the actor's id, email, and role onto the event at write time instead of relying on a join, both because cold-storage queries can't join against your users table and because a user deleted under GDPR must not take their audit trail with them. No foreign keys from audit to application tables, for the same reason.

For tamper-evidence inside the hot window, give the application an insert-only role with UPDATE and DELETE revoked on the audit schema, and chain each row's hash over the previous one so that a superuser edit is detectable even though it isn't preventable. A separate datastore wouldn't have prevented it either unless it were WORM, which is exactly what the S3 tier gives you once records land there.

The case for a dedicated store is strongest if your auditors have specifically demanded physical separation, or if you expect to query audit events analytically at high volume rather than looking up individual investigations. Neither sounds like your situation, and the join requirement you mentioned points the other way, since support investigations against recent events stay in one database and one query.
