I'd keep it in Postgres, but not in the same database as your application data. Give it its own database on its own instance, partition by month, hold a hot window of a year or two, and export older partitions to object storage. That gets you the operational isolation the separate-store camp wants without giving up SQL or introducing a second technology your team has to learn to investigate an incident at 2am.

The volume doesn't force you out of Postgres. Two million events a day is about twenty-five writes per second averaged out, and even with a peaky distribution that's nothing. Cumulatively you're looking at roughly 5.1 billion rows and somewhere between two and five terabytes depending on payload size, which is a lot for one table and unremarkable for a partitioned one: monthly partitions land around sixty million rows each, you'll have eighty-odd of them, and no realistic query touches more than a few, so the storage engine isn't what should push this decision either way.

What does argue for separation is blast radius and lifecycle. If the audit log lives beside application data, a multi-terabyte table sits on the same volume, in the same base backup, on the same replication stream, and in the same restore. Your recovery time for the database that actually serves customers becomes dominated by data you read a handful of times a month for support tickets. The same coupling shows up in major-version upgrades, in autovacuum freeze pressure across billions of tuples, and in a clumsy support query evicting your working set from shared buffers during peak traffic. None of these is catastrophic on its own, but together they let the audit log's growth curve set the operational tempo for your most important database.

The join requirement is the real argument for co-location, and it's weaker than it looks. An audit event should record the actor's identity as it stood at the time of the event: user id, email, role, org, whatever your investigators need, denormalised into the row. Joining to live user records gives you current values, which for forensics is frequently the wrong answer, because the person changed their email or moved between orgs after the event you're investigating. Once identity is denormalised, most investigations become single-table queries. For the residual case where you need to filter on something only the application database knows, such as every event from accounts on a particular plan, `postgres_fdw` between two Postgres instances handles it in the SQL your team already writes. Keeping the same engine on both sides is what preserves the join; keeping the same instance is not.

Before you commit, settle what "append-only" means to whoever asked for it, because the two readings lead to different architectures. If it's an engineering property, Postgres gives you nearly all of it: the writing role holds INSERT and nothing else, UPDATE and DELETE are revoked, and retention is enforced by a separate maintenance role detaching whole partitions rather than deleting rows. If it's a compliance requirement for tamper-evident or WORM storage, no Postgres configuration satisfies it, since anyone with superuser can rewrite history without leaving a trace. Object storage with a retention lock does satisfy it, S3 Object Lock in compliance mode or your provider's equivalent, and if that's what your auditors want then the archive tier is your system of record rather than a cost optimisation, with Postgres demoted to a queryable index over it.

Build the archive tier regardless. Detaching partitions past the hot window and writing them out as compressed Parquet takes steady-state database size from several terabytes to well under one, cuts storage cost by roughly an order of magnitude over a seven-year horizon, and puts the WORM guarantee within reach if you later need it. The part to get right is the restore path, which should be a documented procedure you rehearse once or twice a year rather than something you improvise while a regulator waits.

One schema note, since seven years means reading rows written by a version of your application that no longer exists. Keep a small set of stable typed columns, roughly event time, actor, action, target, and source, and put everything else in a JSONB payload with a GIN index if your query patterns need one. Adding a typed column across billions of rows four years from now is a migration nobody wants to run; adding a key to a payload costs nothing.
