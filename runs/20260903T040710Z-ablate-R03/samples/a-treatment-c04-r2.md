I'd keep it in the same Postgres instance, in a separate schema with insert-only grants and monthly partitioning, and tier partitions older than roughly a year out to object storage. The join requirement is what decides it, and the volume numbers are less alarming than they sound.

Start with the arithmetic, because it reframes the argument. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second averaged out, call it a few hundred per second at peak. Postgres will not notice that; a single modest instance handles an order of magnitude more. What you actually have is a storage lifecycle problem: seven years at that rate is around 5 billion rows, and at a realistic 500 bytes to 1KB per event with indexes you are looking at several terabytes. Shipping to a separate append-only store does not make those terabytes go away. It relocates them and adds a system boundary, so the question is whether the boundary buys you more than it costs.

For your workload it mostly costs. Support investigations that join audit events against user records are the common case you'll run every week, and a cross-system join means either exporting user data into the audit store, pulling audit events back into a scratch table, or standing up a federated query layer. All three turn a thirty-second query into a small project, and the people running those investigations are usually the ones least equipped to write glue code under time pressure. Keeping both sides in one database preserves the ordinary path.

You can get the properties people want from "append-only" without leaving Postgres. Put the audit tables in their own schema, grant the application role INSERT only, and revoke UPDATE and DELETE from everything except a maintenance role used for partition management. If the compliance requirement includes tamper evidence rather than just immutability, hash-chain the rows: each event carries a hash over its own contents plus the previous event's hash, which is cheap to write and makes silent edits detectable even by someone with superuser access. One design detail matters more than it looks: do not put a foreign key with cascading delete from user records to audit rows, because the log has to survive user deletion. Store the user id alongside a denormalized snapshot of the identifying fields, so an event stays meaningful after the referenced row is gone, and the support join becomes a convenience rather than a dependency.

The legitimate worry in the separate-store camp is operational coupling, and tiering answers it. A five-terabyte database drags your backup window and, more importantly, your restore time for application data, which is the number that matters during an incident. Partition by month, keep twelve to eighteen months attached and live, and detach older partitions on a schedule, writing them out as Parquet to object storage with a WORM policy such as S3 Object Lock. Your hot database stays under a terabyte and restores quickly; the seven-year obligation is satisfied by storage an auditor will recognize; and the rare query against year-five data runs through Athena or DuckDB on a slower path, which is proportionate to how often anyone asks.

Two things would change my answer. If your auditor requires administrative separation, meaning the people who can modify the production database must not be able to modify the audit trail, then the coupling is precisely what you're being asked to eliminate and the log belongs in a system with independent credentials. And if you expect volume to grow by an order of magnitude within the retention window, the partition count and hot-tier sizing stop working and you should design for the destination now rather than migrate 5 billion rows later.
