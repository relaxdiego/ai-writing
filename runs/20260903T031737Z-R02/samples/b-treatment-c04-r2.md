I'd keep it in Postgres, but not as an ordinary table in your application schema, and not with all seven years living there. The split your team is having is usually a proxy for two different questions that have different answers, and separating them dissolves most of the disagreement.

The first question is whether audit data needs stronger integrity guarantees than application data. At 2M events a day the write load is nothing — roughly 23 events per second average, maybe a couple hundred at peak, which Postgres will not notice. What a separate store buys you is not throughput but the inability of your application to rewrite history. You can get most of that inside Postgres by putting audit events in their own schema owned by a role your application cannot use, granting the app role INSERT and SELECT only, and revoking UPDATE and DELETE entirely. A superuser or someone with direct DB access can still tamper, so if your compliance regime actually demands cryptographic tamper-evidence or WORM media — SEC 17a-4, certain financial and healthcare regimes — then this is the argument that wins and you should ship to a store designed for it. If the requirement is the more common "maintain a complete audit trail with defined retention," grant-level enforcement plus a hash chain over event rows is generally what auditors are looking for, and it costs you a day of work instead of a new operational system.

The second question is where the data lives across seven years, and here the answer should not be "in your primary cluster." Five point one billion rows at a plausible 500 bytes to 1KB each lands you somewhere between 2.5 and 5TB before indexes. Your primary Postgres does not care much about that at query time if you partition by month, but your backup and restore story cares enormously — a 5TB cluster has a very different recovery time objective than a 500GB one, and during the incident where you actually need to restore, you will be waiting on seven years of audit log you have no use for. Partition monthly, keep a hot window in the database, and detach older partitions out to object storage as Parquet where you can query them with DuckDB or Athena when an auditor asks. The hot window should be sized by your actual support workload; if investigations almost never reach past a year, twelve months keeps you around 700M rows and a manageable few hundred gigabytes, and reattaching an archived partition for an unusual request is a routine operation rather than an emergency.

The join requirement is the argument your co-location camp is leaning on, and it is weaker than it looks. Joining a 2021 audit event against today's user row tells you what that user's name and role are *now*, which is frequently the wrong answer and occasionally a misleading one — the whole point of an audit trail is what was true at the time. You want to denormalize the actor's identity, email, role, and tenant into the event at write time, as a snapshot, and this is correct practice whether or not the data shares a database. Once you have done that, the majority of support investigations need no join at all. The ones that genuinely do — "show me every event by users who currently belong to this org" — are served fine by the hot partitions being local, and for the archived tail you can push a small list of user IDs into the object-store query rather than joining across systems.

Where I would change my recommendation: if you already run a log pipeline with Kafka and a downstream sink, the marginal cost of a separate audit store drops to near zero and the isolation is worth taking. If your event volume is on a trajectory toward 20M a day rather than 2M, the hot window shrinks until Postgres is doing nothing useful for you. And if there is any chance a regulator will ask for cryptographic proof of immutability, decide that now — retrofitting a hash chain across five billion existing rows is a project, whereas writing one from day one is a column and a trigger.

The concrete shape I'd propose to the team: `audit` schema, insert-only grants, monthly partitions on event time, denormalized actor snapshot, a `prev_hash`/`hash` pair per row, twelve months hot, older partitions detached to Parquet in S3 with lifecycle rules carrying them to the seven-year mark. It gives the isolation camp real integrity and blast-radius separation, gives the co-location camp real SQL joins over the window they actually query, and avoids committing you to operating a second stateful system for a workload that is 23 writes a second.
