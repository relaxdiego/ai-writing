I'd separate it — but the version of "separate" I'd pick is probably less dramatic than what your team is arguing about, and the reason isn't the one usually given.

**The scale, first.** Two million events a day is 730 million rows a year and roughly 5.1 billion over the retention window. Write throughput is a non-issue: 23 events/second average, maybe a few hundred at peak, which Postgres will not notice. The problem is entirely the resting mass. At 500 bytes to 1 KB per event — realistic if you're storing a JSON payload of what changed — you're looking at 2.5–5 TB of heap plus indexes, so call it 4–8 TB. Putting that on your primary means your application database's backup size, restore time, replication bandwidth, storage bill, and major-version upgrade window all get dominated by data that has an RTO of "sometime next week." When your app database goes down at 3 a.m., you want to restore the 200 GB that customers are waiting on, not wait through terabytes of audit history you have no urgent need for. The RPO and RTO of audit data are genuinely different from those of application data, and colocating them forces the strict requirement onto both.

**The join requirement is weaker than it looks.** This is usually the argument that wins the room for colocation, but an audit event that stores only `user_id` and joins to a live `users` table is quietly wrong. If someone is renamed, changes role, transfers teams, or is deleted under a GDPR request, your audit trail retroactively rewrites history and now claims the action was taken by whoever that row describes today. What you actually want is a point-in-time snapshot denormalized into the event at write time — actor ID, email, display name, role, tenant, and the IP or session it came from. Once you've done that, which you should do regardless of where the data lives, the join you needed for support investigations mostly evaporates. What remains is the occasional "show me everything this user did" query, which is an indexed lookup on a denormalized column rather than a relational join.

**The immutability requirement is stronger than a shared database can honor.** You can revoke UPDATE and DELETE from the application role, add triggers, and get most of the way there, but a superuser or anyone with migration rights can still alter the table, and there's no artifact you can hand an auditor that demonstrates otherwise. How much this matters depends entirely on your regime — SOX or PCI with an external auditor is a different conversation from an internal retention policy — so it's worth pinning down before the design argument goes further. If you do need defensible immutability, object storage with S3 Object Lock in compliance mode gives you something a shared Postgres cluster structurally cannot.

**The real cost of separating, which nobody has raised yet.** If audit lives elsewhere, you lose the ability to write the audit event in the same transaction as the change it describes, and you're now in dual-write territory where a crash between the two leaves you with a mutation nobody recorded. The fix is the transactional outbox: the application writes the audit event to a small outbox table inside the same transaction as the business change, and a relay process ships rows from there to the audit store and deletes them once acknowledged. You get atomicity at the write, at-least-once delivery downstream, and an outbox table that stays tiny because it drains continuously. The tax is one more moving piece to operate and monitor for lag, which I think is worth paying — and it's the honest counterweight your colocation advocates should be arguing from, rather than the join story.

**Concretely, what I'd build.** Keep audit in Postgres so you're not adopting a new query language and a new operational skill set on day one, but give it its own instance and its own database. Partition monthly by event time, denormalize actor and target attributes into each row, index on `(actor_id, occurred_at)` and `(target_type, target_id, occurred_at)`, and feed it through an outbox relay from the application. Once a partition is a few months past and cold, roll it out to Parquet in object storage with Object Lock and detach it — retention expiry then becomes dropping a partition rather than a 700-million-row DELETE that vacuum will fight for weeks. Support investigations hit the hot Postgres window and stay fast; the rare seven-year-old query goes to the archive through Athena or DuckDB and is allowed to take thirty seconds.

If it turns out the compliance requirement is soft and there's no external auditor, a monthly-partitioned table inside the application database is defensible and I wouldn't call it a mistake — Postgres handles 5 billion partitioned rows fine. The backup-and-restore coupling is what would still push me out, independent of any compliance argument at all.
