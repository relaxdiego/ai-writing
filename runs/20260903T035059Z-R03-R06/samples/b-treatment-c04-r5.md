I'd ship it to a separate store, and the number that decides it is 5.1 billion rows. Two million events a day over seven years, at a few hundred bytes to a kilobyte each once you count the actor, target, timestamp, source IP and a JSON payload, lands somewhere between three and six terabytes with indexes. The write rate itself is nothing: twenty-five events a second on average, a couple hundred at peak, is a rounding error for Postgres. Throughput is not the reason to split. The reason is that several terabytes of write-once data permanently changes the operational character of the database your application depends on to serve requests.

Concretely, that data lengthens every base backup and every restore, so your recovery time objective for the application ends up governed by rows no runtime code path ever reads. It rides along on every replica you provision for high availability, and on every clone you spin up for staging. It expands the surface that autovacuum has to walk, and it makes any schema migration touching the audit table a genuinely frightening operation rather than a routine one. Seven-year retention also implies eventual expiry, and deleting a year's worth of rows out of a shared table is a vacuum and bloat event you will feel in application latency, whereas dropping a detached monthly partition is close to free.

There's a second argument that matters more for compliance than for operations. Append-only inside your application database is a policy, not a property. You can revoke UPDATE and DELETE from the app role, add triggers, and separate credentials, and that is meaningfully better than nothing, but any migration, any superuser session, any ORM running with elevated grants can undo it, and the auditor's question is whether tampering is prevented or merely discouraged. Object storage with a retention lock, or an equivalent WORM-configured store, answers that question differently, because the immutability lives below the layer your engineers operate.

The join requirement is the weakest part of the case for co-location, and I'd argue it partly cuts the other way. Support investigations are low-frequency, latency-tolerant and almost always scoped to one subject over a bounded window, which is a lookup rather than a join. More importantly, an audit record that stores only `user_id` and resolves the identity against a mutable users table is not really a durable record: if the user changes their email or the row is deleted, your history of what happened in 2021 silently changes. Audit events should carry a denormalized snapshot of the actor and target as they were at the moment of the event, which makes cross-referencing an enrichment step rather than a correctness dependency. Worth checking before you commit to that, though, is how your privacy counsel treats erasure requests against audit data; the usual position is that a legal retention obligation overrides erasure, but you want that settled before you write five billion immutable rows containing email addresses.

The strongest honest argument for keeping it in Postgres is transactional atomicity: writing the audit row in the same transaction as the business change means the record and the effect commit or roll back together, and a separate store gives you crash windows where you log something that never happened or lose something that did. You don't have to give that up. Write into a small outbox or staging table in the application database inside the same transaction, then have a shipper drain it into the audit store and delete on acknowledgement. The staging table stays in the thousands of rows, you keep exactly-once-ish semantics with idempotency keys, and the durable copy still lives elsewhere.

The shape I'd build is a hot tier of ninety to a hundred and eighty days in its own Postgres cluster, partitioned monthly on event time, written by an insert-only role, which covers essentially every support investigation with ordinary SQL and lets you attach the users table by foreign data wrapper if you want the convenience. Older partitions get detached, exported to Parquet in object storage under an object-lock retention policy covering the full seven years, and queried on the rare occasion by DuckDB or Athena. Your primary carries a small outbox instead of terabytes of history.

The asymmetry is what settles it if the team is still split. Starting co-located and discovering at year three that you need to migrate five billion rows off a live primary is a project; starting separated and discovering it was overkill costs you one extra cluster to operate. Below roughly a tenth of this volume, or with retention measured in months, I'd give the opposite answer and keep it in one place.
