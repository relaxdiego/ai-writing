I'd keep it in Postgres, but not as one undifferentiated seven-year table sitting next to your application tables, and I'd say the split on your team is probably a disagreement about two separable questions that have been fused into one.

The strongest argument for co-location is transactional integrity of the write. If the audit insert happens in the same transaction as the change it describes, the audit record exists if and only if the change committed, and you get that guarantee for free from the database. Ship to a separate store and you are dual-writing: the application change commits, the network call to the audit service fails, and now you have an unlogged privileged action, which is precisely the case an auditor cares about. The usual fix is an outbox table in Postgres plus a shipper process, but notice what that costs you. You are now writing to Postgres anyway, running a relay, and operating a second system, and you have bought nothing that Postgres wasn't already giving you. At two million events a day you are averaging around twenty-three inserts per second, so perhaps a couple of hundred at peak. That is not a volume that pushes Postgres anywhere near its limits; append-only tables with no updates are also the friendliest possible workload for autovacuum.

The join requirement is a weaker argument than it looks, and I'd examine it before leaning on it. An audit event from 2019 should record the actor's email, role and display name as they were in 2019, denormalized into the event row, because joining to the current user table tells you who that person is today rather than who they were when they did the thing. Once you denormalize the identity fields you need, the residual value of a live join is convenience during investigations, not correctness. Keep the user id as a plain column with no foreign key, since you do not want a user deletion to cascade into your audit trail or to be blocked by it.

Where I'd side with the separate-store camp is on everything the word "separate" is standing in for. Put the audit tables in their own schema with their own role. Grant the application role INSERT and nothing else, and revoke UPDATE and DELETE explicitly, so append-only is enforced by the database rather than by convention. That gets you most of what people imagine they're buying from an append-only product. Shipping to a different system does not by itself make data immutable; permissions and storage-level write-once controls do.

Then partition by month and stop keeping all seven years hot. Rough sizing: at a kilobyte per event you're looking at about two gigabytes a day, three-quarters of a terabyte a year, five terabytes plus index overhead across the retention window. Postgres can hold that, but you'll feel it in the places people forget to check: restore time when you need to recover application data, the length of your point-in-time recovery window, the cost of provisioned SSD on your primary, and the initial sync time for a new replica. Keep twelve to eighteen months attached, which covers essentially every support investigation, and detach older partitions on a schedule, exporting them to Parquet in object storage with a lifecycle policy and object lock for the remainder of the seven years. Compressed columnar files will hold the whole archive in a few hundred gigabytes, cost almost nothing, and stay queryable through DuckDB or Athena when someone genuinely needs 2021. The one discipline this requires is testing the read path for archived data before you need it, because an archive nobody has ever restored from is not an archive.

Two things would change my answer. If your compliance regime names an independent, externally attested system rather than describing controls and retention periods, then the regulator has made the decision for you and the engineering tradeoff is moot; worth reading the actual control language rather than someone's summary of it. And if your threat model includes an attacker who has compromised the application database and you need the audit trail to survive that, co-location fails by construction, though the cheaper answer there is usually hash-chaining the events and periodically anchoring the chain head somewhere the database cannot reach, rather than moving the whole store.

So: one database, separate schema and role, INSERT-only grants, monthly partitions, eighteen months online, the rest in object storage under lock. You keep the transactional write guarantee, you keep support investigations easy, and you get the isolation the other half of the team is actually asking for without operating a second system to get it.
