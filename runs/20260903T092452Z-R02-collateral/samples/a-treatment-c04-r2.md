Keep it in Postgres, in the same database under its own schema, partitioned by month and hardened with permissions rather than moved to a different store. Move it out later if you actually hit the wall, because that migration is far cheaper than unwinding a bespoke audit pipeline.

Volume is not what should decide this. Two million events a day averages about twenty-three writes per second, a few hundred at peak, which is unremarkable for Postgres and would stay unremarkable at ten times the rate. The pressure comes from accumulation: roughly 5.1 billion rows over seven years, somewhere between two and eight terabytes depending on how fat your event payloads are, living in a database whose backup window, restore time, and major-version upgrades you would rather keep short. An unpartitioned audit table is what turns that into a real problem. A monthly-partitioned one mostly does not, because old partitions are immutable, compress well, get vacuumed once and never again, and can be detached and archived to object storage on a schedule without touching the live query path.

The append-only property is something you enforce, not something you buy. Revoke `UPDATE` and `DELETE` on the audit schema from every role except a dedicated writer, give the writer `INSERT` only, and add a `BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE` trigger that raises. Ship WAL to a separate retention target so the archive is not governed by the same lifecycle as your application backups. An auditor asking how you guarantee immutability wants to see access controls, change management on those controls, and evidence they hold; a grant table and a trigger definition answer that question about as well as a vendor's marketing page does.

Your join requirement points the same way, though I would not lean on it too hard given you said it is occasional. A separate store does not make joins expensive, it makes them absent: you would be replicating user records into the audit system, or doing two round trips and stitching results in application code. Support investigations are precisely the workload where someone needs to write an unanticipated query against both datasets at once, and preserving that costs you nothing here.

The one thing that would flip this answer is what your compliance regime actually says. If "seven years" comes from SOX, SOC 2, or a GDPR-adjacent retention policy, Postgres with enforced controls is fine and is what most teams in that position run. If it comes from a WORM mandate of the SEC 17a-4 variety, no permission scheme satisfies it, because the requirement is about the storage medium refusing overwrites independently of your database administrators. In that case the design is not either/or: Postgres becomes a queryable hot mirror over the recent window, and S3 with Object Lock in compliance mode holds the record of truth. Worth settling that question before the team argues further, since the two readings lead to different systems.

Concretely, what I would build:

- Audit events in their own schema in the application database, partitioned by `RANGE` on event timestamp, one partition per month, created a few months ahead by a scheduled job.
- Insert-only grants for the writing role, a trigger blocking updates and deletes, and no ORM-managed migrations against those tables.
- A hot window of twelve to eighteen months online. Older partitions detached, exported to Parquet in object storage, and dropped, with a documented restore path for the rare old-record request.
- Separate WAL archiving and retention for the audit schema's lifecycle, so application backup pruning cannot shorten the audit trail.
- Indexes kept deliberately thin: actor, target, and timestamp cover nearly every support query, and each additional index costs you meaningfully at five billion rows.
