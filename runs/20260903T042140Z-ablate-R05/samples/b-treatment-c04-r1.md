I'd split it, but not along the line your team is arguing about. The choice isn't really "one Postgres" versus "one append-only store"; it's hot versus cold, and the two halves of your requirement pull in opposite directions hard enough that trying to serve both from one home is what's making the decision feel contentious.

Start with the volume, because I suspect it's being used as an argument in a way it doesn't support. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes per second on average, maybe a hundred at peak. Postgres will not notice that. Nobody should be choosing an architecture here on write throughput. The actual number that matters is the cumulative one: seven years at that rate is roughly five billion rows, and at a realistic 500 bytes to a kilobyte per event once you include a JSON payload, you are looking at somewhere between three and eight terabytes with indexes. That volume in your application database is what hurts, and it hurts in a specific and underappreciated place, which is recovery. Your application data probably restores in minutes. Bolt a multi-terabyte audit archive onto the same cluster and your disaster recovery time is now governed by data that no user-facing request will ever touch. You also inherit the archive in every base backup, every replica rebuild, and every major-version upgrade rehearsal, and you inherit its autovacuum and bloat behaviour on the same instance that serves your checkout path.

Against that, the same-database camp has one argument that is genuinely strong and usually gets stated badly. If the audit row is written in the same transaction as the mutation it describes, then an action cannot commit without its audit record. Ship events over the wire to a separate store and you have introduced a delivery problem: at-least-once gives you duplicates, at-most-once gives you gaps, and gaps in an audit log are exactly the thing compliance cares about. Any design that has the application POST to an external audit service and hope is worse than keeping it all in Postgres, whatever the operational cost.

So write into Postgres, into a table declaratively range-partitioned by month, in the same transaction as the change it records. Then run a mover that ages partitions out after a hot window of ninety days or so, detaching each one and writing it to object storage as Parquet under an object-lock retention policy. Recent events, which is what support actually opens, stay in the database where you can join them freely. The seven-year tail lives somewhere that costs a few dollars a terabyte per month, is genuinely immutable in a way an auditor will accept rather than in the approximated way that revoking UPDATE and DELETE grants gives you, and is queryable when you need it through Athena or DuckDB without being on the critical path for anything.

The join requirement mostly dissolves under a change you want to make regardless. An audit event should record the actor's identity as it was at the moment of the event: email, display name, role, tenant, all denormalised into the row. If you instead join to the live users table you will eventually show an investigator the wrong answer, because the user was renamed, changed roles, or was deleted in the two years since. Once those fields are on the event, "occasionally join against user records" turns out to mean "occasionally look up a user by id in a second query," which works fine across a store boundary.

Two things would change my recommendation. If your compliance regime is something like SEC 17a-4 that demands true WORM media, the archive half stops being optional and the hot window should be as short as your support workflow tolerates. Conversely, if the obligation is only seven years retained with retrieval measured in days rather than seconds, lean further cold: shrink the hot window to thirty days and stop worrying about query ergonomics on the tail entirely. Worth pinning down which of those you're actually subject to before the team spends more time on it, because it settles more of the argument than any of the technical points do.
