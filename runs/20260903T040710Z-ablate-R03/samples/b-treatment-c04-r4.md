I'd put the audit log in its own Postgres database, on its own instance, rather than either sharing tables with application data or adopting a separate append-only technology. The question as posed sets up a binary between convenience and rigour, but most of the benefit of separation comes from the isolation itself, and very little of it comes from switching to a different storage engine.

Start with the numbers, because they rule out one argument each side tends to lean on. Seven years at two million events a day is about five billion rows, somewhere in the range of two to five terabytes with indexes depending on payload size. The write rate averages around twenty-five events a second, which Postgres will not notice. So ingest volume is not a reason to leave Postgres. What the size does affect is everything that has to sweep the whole database: your base backups, your restore time objective for application data, autovacuum's workload, the disk you have to provision on every replica, and the pain of any migration that touches a large table. Tying your application's recovery time to a multi-terabyte table you read a few times a month is the cost that compounds, and it is paid during incidents rather than during design.

The append-only property deserves a harder look than teams usually give it. Inside the application database, the same role that writes audit rows can generally update and delete them; you can revoke DML, add a trigger that raises on UPDATE and DELETE, and put writes behind a security-definer function, and for most auditors that is sufficient. If your compliance regime actually demands write-once media rather than logical controls, no arrangement of Postgres permissions satisfies it and you need object storage with a retention lock. Worth confirming which of those two worlds you are in before the architecture argument continues, because it settles a lot of it.

The join requirement is the weakest reason to colocate, and arguably points the other way. An audit event should record who the actor was and what the subject looked like at the time it happened, so joining against a mutable user record gives you today's email address attached to a two-year-old event, which is exactly the misleading answer you don't want in an investigation. Denormalise the identifying attributes into the event at write time. The joins that remain are small-N lookups during support work, fetching fifty audit rows and then the handful of users they reference, and that works fine as two queries from the support tool or through postgres_fdw. It is not a hash join across billions of rows in any realistic case.

Concretely, I'd use declarative partitioning by month, keep something like the most recent eighteen to twenty-four months resident and indexed for support queries, and roll older partitions out to Parquet in object storage under a retention lock, restorable on demand for the rare deep investigation. That gives you cheap storage for years five through seven, a genuine WORM story for the archive, and a hot table that stays a manageable size.

The colocation camp is right about one thing: two databases mean two backup regimes, two upgrade schedules, and a distributed-transaction question at write time, which you resolve by writing the audit row in the request path only after the business transaction commits, and accepting an outbox or a queue if you cannot lose events. If your volume were a tenth of this, or you had no one to run a second cluster, I'd tell you to keep it in one database with partitioning and revoked permissions and revisit in two years. At five billion rows and a seven-year horizon, the separation is easier to do now than it will ever be again.
