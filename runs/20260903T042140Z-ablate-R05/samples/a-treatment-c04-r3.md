I'd keep it in Postgres, but not in the same schema as application data: give the audit log its own schema with its own writer role, partitioned by month, in the same cluster you already run. The reason is that at your volume the arguments for a separate store are weaker than they sound, while the cost of running a second storage technology is real and permanent, and starting in Postgres keeps the separation cheap to do later if you need it.

Start with the numbers, because they set the ceiling on the whole discussion. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes per second averaged out, so call it a few hundred per second at peak. Postgres will not notice that alongside your application traffic. Seven years at that rate is roughly five billion rows, which at a realistic 500 bytes to 1 KB per event lands somewhere between 2.5 and 5 TB before indexes. The volume is not a throughput problem; it is a storage and operations problem, and it shows up in backup duration, restore time, and what you're paying for primary-tier storage to hold data nobody has read in four years. Monthly partitions address most of that: you can put older partitions on a cheaper tablespace, and the seven-year expiry becomes a DROP TABLE instead of a delete that runs for a week and bloats everything it touches.

The join requirement is the argument most often used to settle this, and I think it deserves less weight than your team is probably giving it. Support investigations are interactive, scoped to one user and usually a narrow window, and they return hundreds of rows rather than millions. More importantly, an audit event should record who the actor was at the time it happened, not carry a foreign key that resolves to whoever that account belongs to today. If someone's email changed, or their role was revoked, or the account was merged into another, a live join actively misrepresents the history you're keeping the log to preserve. Denormalising actor identity into the event at write time is better audit design on its own merits, and it happens to remove most of the pressure to keep the two datasets joinable.

What a separate append-only store genuinely buys you is the guarantee that the application cannot rewrite its own history, and you should check whether your auditor requires that specific property or merely requires that you can demonstrate it. In Postgres you get most of the way there by having the application write through a role with INSERT granted and UPDATE and DELETE revoked, with schema ownership held elsewhere. If the requirement is stronger, a hash chain over each event, sealed periodically to external storage, gives you tamper evidence without moving the data. Neither of these is exotic, and both are considerably less work than operating a second system with its own backup story, access controls, monitoring, and on-call knowledge.

The condition that should change my answer is scale. If volume grows an order of magnitude, or if restoring the application database in an incident starts being gated by audit data you'd never need in the first hour, split it out. Because you'd be splitting Postgres to Postgres, that migration is a logical dump of one schema rather than a rewrite of every write path, and the recent partitions that support actually queries can stay where they are while the cold ones move. Building the cheap option first is what preserves that.
