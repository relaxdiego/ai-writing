Ship it to a separate store, but keep it boring: a second Postgres cluster holding the recent window, with everything past that window frozen into immutable object storage.

The join requirement looks like it settles the question for co-location, and it's the argument I'd push back on hardest. An audit record that resolves its actor through a foreign key tells you who that user is now, not who they were when the event happened. Names, emails, roles and team membership all drift, and an account deletion or a GDPR erasure can remove the row the trail depends on, which turns your seven-year history into a wall of nulls. You want actor identity denormalized into the event at write time no matter where the log lives. Once you've done that, the join you were protecting is enrichment rather than reconstruction, and `postgres_fdw` or a replicated copy of the users table covers it for investigations that happen a few times a week.

On the cost side, 2 million events a day is nothing as throughput, roughly 25 writes a second averaged out. The problem is cumulative: about 5.1 billion rows over seven years, somewhere between 3 and 15 TB with indexes depending on how large your payloads are. Sitting in the application cluster, that mass taxes every operation you perform on the data you actually serve. Backup and restore windows, replica rebuild time, major-version upgrades and autovacuum scheduling all scale with it, and recovering the app database after an incident means restoring seven years of audit history first to get back the tables your users need.

Co-location also gives you the word "append-only" without the property. If the application's role can insert into the table, it can usually delete from it too, and a compromised application credential is precisely the scenario the audit log exists to survive. Tamper resistance is a property of the write path and the credential boundary, not the storage engine. A separate cluster where the app holds `INSERT` and nothing else, with cold data under object lock, is a claim you can defend to an auditor.

Concretely:

- **Hot tier.** Six to twelve months in a dedicated Postgres cluster, declaratively partitioned by month. Around 60 million rows per partition, indexed on actor and timestamp, queried directly by support.
- **Cold tier.** Partitions aged out of the hot window exported to Parquet in object storage, with versioning and object lock covering the rest of the retention period, read through DuckDB or Athena when compliance or legal asks.
- **Enrichment.** The users table exposed to the audit cluster by `postgres_fdw` or logical replication, so ad-hoc joins keep working without the log living beside your OLTP data.

What I'd steer away from is a purpose-built ledger product or an Elasticsearch cluster. Retrieval here is rare and tolerant of latency, so you would be paying a search cluster's storage premium across seven years to serve a handful of queries a week, on an operational surface nobody on the team already runs.

Two things would move me. If your events are small and payload-free, on the order of 200 bytes, and your compliance regime doesn't actually mandate write-once media, then a single partitioned table in the application database with a monthly detach-and-archive job is a defensible answer, and the simplicity is worth real money. And if "occasionally" turns out to mean an interactive tool your support team works in all day rather than a few investigations a week, then queryability of the hot window matters more than I've weighted it, though that argues for generous hot-tier retention rather than for the same cluster.
