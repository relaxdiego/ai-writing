A one-in-twenty rate with the location moving around is the most informative thing you have, and it argues against a defect in any individual test. Two families of cause produce that signature. Either something global is being poisoned by an earlier test and the hang lands on whichever test next touches it, or the hang has nothing to do with test identity at all and is an environment-level event that strikes at a random moment. Before you chase either, check how you're determining "which test it hangs at": with parallel workers and buffered output, the last name printed is often just the last line flushed, not the test that's stuck, and several workers may be in flight. Check too whether it hangs mid-suite or after the last test has passed, because a process that runs everything and then won't exit (a non-daemon thread, an open socket keeping the event loop alive, an unclosed pool) presents exactly as "hung at a random test" and has an entirely different fix.

The pool hypothesis is cheap to falsify, so do that first rather than arguing about it. Give the pool a bounded acquisition timeout of a few seconds. If a connection leak is really the mechanism, your 1-in-20 hang becomes a 1-in-20 fast failure with a stack trace pointing at the checkout site, and you're done. If runs keep hanging, the hypothesis is dead and cost you one config line. Two things make me share your doubt: leaks are usually monotonic, so the failure position should drift and correlate with tests-run-so-far rather than scattering, unless your runner randomizes order per run; and pool exhaustion is frequently the misdiagnosis for a dependency that has died, since an unreachable database also looks like "can't get a connection".

That dead-dependency case is worth ruling out early. If the Postgres or Redis container gets OOM-killed by the runner, a client blocked in a socket read with no timeout and no TCP keepalive waits forever. Inspect the exit codes of every service container at the end of the job; 137 is the tell. Related, but at a different layer, is a lock wait rather than a connection wait: parallel workers sharing one database, a transaction left open by an aborted test, and a second worker blocking on a row or advisory lock. Postgres has no lock timeout by default, so it waits indefinitely rather than reporting a deadlock. Setting `lock_timeout` and `statement_timeout` in the CI database converts that class of hang into an error with a query attached.

The reason it never reproduces locally points hard at one specific mechanism: thread or executor starvation from core count. Anything sized off `availableProcessors` or `os.cpu_count` shrinks from your laptop's twelve to a runner's two. A fixed pool whose workers block on tasks queued behind them deadlocks at two threads and never at twelve, and the same arithmetic applies to async runtimes where a blocking call occupies a scarce executor slot. You can test this without touching CI by running the suite locally under `docker run --cpus=1 --memory=2g` or by pinning the pool-size environment variable to the CI value. The other CI-only mechanism is timing: a wait helper that attaches a listener after triggering the action it's waiting for will lose the signal whenever the response beats the registration, which is rare on a fast local machine and common on a jittery shared runner. Grep the integration helpers for any wait, poll, or await with no timeout argument; each one is a candidate site, and giving every one of them a bound is worth doing regardless of which theory wins.

One more possibility to check by correlation rather than by code: shared state between concurrent CI jobs. If your integration tests hit a database, queue, or namespace that isn't per-run isolated, two overlapping runs will take the same advisory lock or consume each other's messages. Pull the start and end timestamps of the hung runs and see whether they overlap another run more often than chance would give.

The way to stop guessing is to make the hang produce evidence instead of a timeout. Add a watchdog to the CI job that fires a few minutes before the job limit and, before killing anything, dumps stacks for every thread in the test process and its children, dumps `pg_stat_activity` including `wait_event` and `pg_locks`, dumps pool gauges, runs `ss -tanp`, and records the state of each service container. The stack dump alone separates every hypothesis above: a frame inside pool checkout means the pool, a frame in query execution with a blocking PID in `pg_locks` means the lock, all executor threads parked with a non-empty queue means starvation, a socket read against a container that exited 137 means the dependency died, and a block in `write` on fd 1 means the log pipe filled and nobody drained it. Getting that dump is per-language plumbing: SIGQUIT for a JVM or Go process, `faulthandler.dump_traceback_later` or `py-spy dump` for Python, `node --report-on-signal` for Node, which also lists the libuv handles keeping the loop alive. Collect it as a job artifact so that the next occurrence, roughly twenty runs from now, answers the question by itself.
