I didn't find a definitive cause, so here's what the evidence does and doesn't support.

The clearest signal is in the run history: the short runs aren't randomly scattered but land on Aug 23, 26, and 29 — a three-day spacing across the eight runs I pulled. On those days the job wrote roughly 22–24k rows against roughly 48k on the other days, and it also finished in about seven minutes against about fifteen. Both halving together matters, because a job that crashed partway through writing, or lost rows in the transaction, would still have spent the full time fetching and grouping. Runtime scaling down with row count points at the job seeing about half as much input, which moves suspicion upstream to the window computation or to `fetch_events`, and away from `write_rollup` and the transaction block. Every one of those runs is also recorded as `ok`, so nothing raised — whatever is happening looks like normal completion over a smaller set.

What I can't explain is the three-day period. Nothing in `scheduler.py` has a three-day cycle in it; `window_start` is `last_success_at or (now - timedelta(hours=24))`, and since every run succeeds, `last_success_at` should always be set and the window should always be about 24 hours. Either `last_success_at` isn't being written on every run, or it's being written with a value that doesn't mean what the fallback assumes, or the halving is happening inside `fetch_events` rather than in the window at all. I didn't read `fetch_events`, and that's the biggest gap in what I looked at.

A few other caveats on what I checked. The count query returned 30, which is also the limit I gave it, so I don't actually know whether `job_runs` holds only 30 rows or is being trimmed — I can't confirm the three-day pattern extends backwards without pulling the full table. I looked at eight runs, which is three short ones, and that's thin evidence for a period. I also pulled `git log` without dates, so I can't say whether `9d02e1e` ("retry transient fetch errors") landed before or after the pattern started, and a retry wrapper around the fetch path is exactly the kind of change that could silently return a partial result instead of raising.

The next things worth doing, roughly in order of how much they'd narrow it down: log `window_start` and `window_end` on every run and compare a short day to a normal one, since that single fact would either confirm or kill the window hypothesis outright; read `fetch_events` along with the retry commit, looking for pagination, a row cap, or a swallowed error that returns what it has; check what actually writes `last_success_at` and whether it runs unconditionally; and for one short day, count the rows genuinely present in the rollup table to confirm `rows_written` is reporting honestly rather than the job having written more than it recorded. If the window turns out to be a full 24 hours on a short day, the cause is upstream of this job entirely and the events source is the place to look next.
