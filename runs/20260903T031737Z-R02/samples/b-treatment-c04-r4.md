I'd split the storage but not the technology: a separate Postgres instance dedicated to audit, partitioned by month, fed through a transactional outbox in the application database. That keeps SQL and joins available while decoupling the two systems where coupling actually hurts.

The reason to separate isn't write throughput. Two million events a day averages about 23 writes per second, which Postgres will not notice. The problem is accumulated mass: roughly 5.1 billion rows over seven years, and at a kilobyte per event with a JSON payload you're looking at five-plus terabytes before indexes. That mass lands on the parts of your operational life that are already painful. Your base backups and PITR window now carry audit data you will essentially never restore under time pressure, so your recovery objective for the application gets set by data nobody is waiting on. Major-version upgrades get slower and riskier. Your primary sits on expensive fast storage sized for a workload that is 95% cold archive. None of these are throughput problems, which is why the "Postgres can handle it" argument tends to win the meeting and then lose the year.

The counterargument usually rests on the join requirement, and I think that requirement is weaker than it appears once you look at what support is actually doing. Investigations concentrate almost entirely in recent history — days to weeks, occasionally months. More importantly, joining a four-year-old audit event against a *current* user row gives you the wrong answer: it tells you what the user's email and role are today, not what they were when the event fired. An audit record that depends on mutable external state to be interpreted isn't really an audit record. So you want to denormalize the actor and subject snapshot into the event at write time regardless of where it lives, which removes most of the join pressure. What's left — "show me all events for this user, then look them up" — is satisfied by keeping a hot window (say 90 days) that's cheap to query, and by the fact that you're still in Postgres and can reach across with a foreign data wrapper or a small replicated user table when you need to.

I'd avoid the pure append-only-store version of the idea for two reasons. The write path is the serious one: if audit lands in a different system via an asynchronous ship, you lose atomicity between the business change and its audit record, and "we mostly log everything" is a bad sentence to say to an auditor. The outbox pattern fixes this — the event is written to a table in the application DB inside the same transaction as the change, then drained to the audit store by a separate process, so the guarantee is at-least-once with the commit boundary intact and the outbox table stays small because rows leave after shipping. The second reason is ecosystem risk. Niche managed immutable-ledger products have a poor track record; AWS deprecated QLDB not long after people built on it. Plain Postgres with a dedicated write role that has INSERT but no UPDATE or DELETE, monthly partitions, and optionally a hash chain over each event gives you a defensible immutability story without betting compliance on a product roadmap.

Two details worth settling early. Partitioning by month is non-negotiable in either design, because at year seven your retention policy becomes a DROP of an 84th partition rather than a DELETE of hundreds of millions of rows that will fight the autovacuum for days. And decide now whether seven years is a floor or a ceiling — many regimes require deletion after the retention period, not merely permit it, and that turns "we kept everything forever, just in case" from prudence into a finding. Beyond the hot window, exporting older partitions to compressed columnar files in object storage cuts the cost of the long tail by an order of magnitude and keeps them queryable when an investigation genuinely reaches back that far.

If the team wants a decision rule rather than a design: the question isn't whether Postgres can hold the data, it's whether you want your audit archive to share a backup, an upgrade window, and a storage bill with the system your customers are waiting on.
