I'd separate it — but less far than "ship it to an append-only store," and the reason has less to do with volume than with the join requirement, which I think is pointing the opposite direction from how your team is reading it.

**Start with the join.** If a support investigation joins a 2023 audit event against the *current* users row, you get the wrong answer: the user's email, role, department, and manager have all changed since. An audit record is supposed to tell you what was true at the time the event happened. So the audit event should carry a denormalized snapshot of the actor and target — user id plus email, display name, role, tenant, whatever your investigations actually key on — captured at write time. Once you do that, which most compliance regimes will push you toward anyway, the live join is a convenience rather than a requirement, and the strongest argument for co-location dissolves. You keep the user id for the occasional "show me everything this person ever did," which is a single-column lookup you can satisfy without a relational join.

**Then the volume.** 2M/day is nothing as a write rate — roughly 23/sec. The problem is the accumulation: about 5 billion rows and, at a plausible 500B–1KB per event, 2.5–5 TB before indexes. That's not too big for Postgres, but it's too big to sit next to your application data, because it silently degrades operations you care about more. Your backups, your PITR restore time, your major-version upgrade window, and your replica rebuild all get dominated by a table nobody queries in the hot path. The failure mode isn't "audit log is slow," it's "the outage lasted four hours because restoring the app database meant restoring 4 TB of audit history."

**So: same technology, different instance.** Postgres for the audit store, declaratively partitioned by month, with older partitions detached and exported to Parquet in object storage once they age past your active investigation window — 12 to 18 months is usually right. Query the archive with DuckDB or Athena when someone needs 2021. You keep SQL, your existing operational skills, and your existing tooling; you don't take on a second data technology to satisfy a requirement that partitioning already handles.

**Make append-only real.** Postgres won't give you immutability by default. The writer role gets INSERT and nothing else — no UPDATE, no DELETE, revoked explicitly. Retention deletion happens by dropping whole partitions under a separate administrative role. If your regime is strict (SOX, HIPAA, financial), add a hash chain over each row so tampering is detectable, and put the archived Parquet files in a bucket with object lock set to seven years, which gives you genuine WORM in a way a database never will.

The one thing that would change my answer: if your compliance auditors have already blessed a specific vendor store, take it. Don't relitigate a settled control.
