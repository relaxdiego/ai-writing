Separate store, with a caveat that changes the shape of the decision: make it a separate Postgres instance rather than a different technology.

Start from the volume. Two million events a day for seven years is roughly five billion rows, and at 300 bytes to 1 KB apiece once you include a JSON diff of what changed, that is somewhere between two and ten terabytes before indexes. Living in your primary OLTP instance, that mass is not really a query problem; a monthly-partitioned, append-only table with a BRIN index on the timestamp barely disturbs the working set of your application queries. It is an operations problem. Base backups grow by the full amount, restore time grows with them, and your recovery objective for the application now includes rehydrating years of audit history that nobody will read during an outage. Major-version upgrades get slower and more frightening. Any logical replication you run has to carry the audit churn alongside the business tables. And the first time you need to change the audit schema, you are running DDL against a multi-terabyte table inside the database that also serves checkout.

None of that argues for leaving Postgres. Eighty-four monthly partitions of sixty million rows each, written in timestamp order and queried by time range, is an unremarkable workload for it, and it keeps the support investigation in SQL. Ship the events to Elasticsearch or a log SaaS and that occasional join against user records turns into an export-and-correlate exercise that someone does by hand at the worst possible moment.

Better, though, to remove most of those joins at the source. An audit record that says "user 4711 updated the invoice" and depends on a mutable users table to say who 4711 was is a weak audit record: the row it points at can be renamed, re-roled, or deleted, and your history of what happened quietly changes with it. Capture the actor's identifier, email, display name and role as they stood at the moment of the event, denormalized into the row. That is what a reviewer actually wants to see, and it collapses the common support query into a single-table scan. For the residual cases that genuinely need current application state, either reach across with postgres_fdw or logically replicate the handful of small dimension tables into the audit database, where they can be joined locally.

The strongest argument your same-database faction has is atomicity, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal. If the audit write is in the same transaction as the business change, you cannot commit one without the other; ship over a queue instead and you have a dual-write problem, losing events on crash or recording events for transactions that rolled back. Resolve it with a transactional outbox: write the audit event into an outbox table in the application database inside the business transaction, then have a relay process move rows to the audit instance and mark them shipped. You keep the atomicity guarantee and still get the storage separation.

Two things worth checking before you commit. First, whether your compliance regime requires WORM media specifically rather than merely durable retention; SEC 17a-4 does, and if you are under something like it, the destination is object storage with object lock and the design question becomes what you keep online in front of it. Second, that nobody is treating "append-only store" as a synonym for tamper-evident. Most are not, and a credentialed operator can do as much damage there as in Postgres. If tamper evidence matters, it comes from revoking UPDATE and DELETE, giving the audit instance its own credentials that the application service account does not hold, and hash-chaining each row to its predecessor with periodic anchoring of the chain head, all of which you can do in Postgres.

For the older partitions, detach on a rolling basis past twelve or eighteen months and write them to compressed Parquet in object storage, keeping the detached partitions readable by DuckDB or Athena for the rare seven-year-old lookup. The asymmetry in cost is what settles the argument for me: splitting now costs a connection string and a relay process, while splitting in three years costs a multi-terabyte migration out of a database you cannot take offline.
