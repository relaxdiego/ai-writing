I'd ship it out, but I'd do it in a way that preserves the property the "same database" camp is actually defending. Their real requirement is usually not colocation but atomicity: the audit event should exist if and only if the thing it describes happened, and a remote write outside the transaction can fail after the commit has already succeeded. An outbox gives that back. Write the event to a small Postgres table in the same transaction as the application change, then have a relay drain it into the audit store and delete the row. The outbox holds minutes of backlog rather than years, and the durability guarantee is identical to storing the log inline.

The retention math is what decides the rest. Seven years at 2M/day is roughly five billion rows. The write rate is nothing, about twenty-five a second on average, and Postgres will not notice it; the accumulated volume is the problem, and it's operational rather than a matter of throughput. It dominates your backup size and your restore time, it stretches every major-version upgrade, its dead tuples compete with application tables for autovacuum workers, and it places a multi-terabyte table inside the blast radius of every cluster incident that has nothing to do with auditing. Coupling your application's recovery time to seven years of compliance data is a poor trade, and it's one that quietly gets worse each year without anyone choosing to make it again.

Separation also buys something auditors tend to ask about that a shared table cannot really provide. In the application database, anyone who can write application data can generally UPDATE the audit rows. You can revoke privileges and add triggers, but a superuser or a stray migration defeats that. A distinct store with distinct credentials, where the application's role may append and do nothing else, is an actual separation of duties rather than a convention everyone agrees to respect.

The join requirement is the weakest argument for colocation, though it doesn't sound like it. If you join audit events against current user records during an investigation, you are reconstructing history from mutable state, and you will get the wrong answer whenever someone's email, role or team changed after the event. What belongs on the audit event is a denormalized snapshot: the actor id together with the name, email and role as they stood at write time, plus the ids you would otherwise have joined on. Most investigations then become single-store lookups, and the answers are correct in a way the join never was. For the residue where you genuinely need current records, keeping a hot window in a Postgres instance dedicated to audit (ninety days covers nearly all support work) lets you do a two-step lookup or a foreign data wrapper query at investigation frequency, where a slow query costs nothing.

For the older tail, partition monthly and export detached partitions to Parquet on object storage, queryable through Athena or DuckDB. Seven-year-old audit data gets read a handful of times a year under legal request, so paying to keep it on hot block storage with indexes spends real money and real restore time on an access pattern you don't have. Compressed columnar files run to tens of gigabytes and satisfy the retention obligation just as well as live rows do.

The one variant I'd rule out is the version of "separate store" where events are fired off asynchronously with no transactional link to the write they describe. That's how you end up with an audit log missing exactly the events from the incident you're investigating, and the outbox is what keeps the separation from costing you that.
