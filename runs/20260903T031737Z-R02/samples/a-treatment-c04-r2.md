I'd keep it in Postgres — but as a partitioned, hardened, tiered table rather than just another table in the app schema. The separate-store instinct is usually reaching for a property that a different database doesn't actually confer, and it costs you the one capability you named as a real requirement.

Start with the sizing, because it's the thing people get wrong in both directions. Two million events a day is 23 writes per second averaged, maybe 100–150 at peak. Postgres will not notice that; it's a rounding error against your application's own write load. The pressure is entirely in the accumulated volume: seven years is 5.1 billion rows, and at a plausible 1KB per event with a JSON payload you're looking at roughly 5TB, give or take a factor of two depending on how fat your payloads are. Nothing about that number threatens query performance on a properly partitioned table, but it does threaten everything that has to traverse the whole database — base backups, restore time, logical replication, major-version upgrades. If your application's recovery-time objective is thirty minutes and 90% of your restore is audit data nobody needs to bring the service back up, you've coupled your RTO to a compliance artifact for no reason.

The tamper-evidence argument deserves more scrutiny than it usually gets, because "append-only store" tends to be treated as a property you acquire by choosing a different system, when it's really a property you construct. S3 is mutable unless you turn on Object Lock; Kafka has retention and compaction; a second Postgres is exactly as mutable as the first one. What actually gives you an auditable trail is a hash chain over event contents, an ingest path that writes through a role holding only INSERT with UPDATE and DELETE revoked, and separation between whoever can write events and whoever can change grants. All three of those are things you can do in your existing database this week, and they're things you'd have to do anyway in a separate store. Moving the data doesn't hand you the guarantee; it just makes the guarantee harder to verify because it now spans two operational domains.

On joins, I'd push back gently on the requirement while still honoring it. An audit event that carries a live foreign key to `users` and resolves the name at read time is subtly wrong for audit purposes, because it shows you who that person is now rather than who they were when they acted — the role they held, the email they used, the tenant they belonged to. Denormalize that state into the event at write time regardless of where you store it. What remains is the support-investigation workflow, and there the value of a single database is real and hard to replicate: someone in a Zoom call with a customer needs to write a join against the events table and three application tables and get an answer in a minute, not export a Parquet file and stand up a query engine. Cross-store investigation tooling always ends up worse than the SQL you'd have written, and it degrades because nobody maintains it between incidents.

So the design I'd build: declarative range partitioning by month on the events table, in its own schema and ideally its own tablespace, with a `BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE` trigger and revoked grants as belt-and-braces immutability, and a `prev_hash`/`hash` pair chained per partition. Keep a hot window in the live database — I'd start at twelve months, which is roughly 730M rows and comfortably queryable, and tighten to ninety days if backup windows start hurting. Detach older partitions on a schedule and write them to object storage as compressed Parquet under Object Lock in compliance mode, keeping a small index table in Postgres that maps actor and time range to archive object, so a seven-year-old investigation is a documented two-step rather than an archaeology project. Verify the chain on a cron and alert on breaks, because an audit log nobody checks is a log nobody can testify about.

The one thing that would flip this: if your compliance regime specifically demands certified WORM media rather than demonstrable integrity — SEC 17a-4 and its relatives, as opposed to SOC 2 or ISO 27001, which accept access controls and hash chaining without complaint — then Postgres cannot satisfy the control no matter how you configure it, and the archive tier becomes the system of record from day one rather than after twelve months. Worth pinning down with whoever owns the audit relationship before the team spends another week split, since it's the only input here that changes the answer rather than the tuning.
