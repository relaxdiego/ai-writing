I'd keep it in Postgres, but not in the same schema as application data, and not for all seven years. Start with the numbers, because they decide more here than the philosophy does. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes a second on average and maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for an instance already serving application traffic. The problem isn't the write rate, it's the accumulation: seven years is roughly five billion rows, somewhere between two and five terabytes with indexes depending on payload size. That volume stays perfectly queryable in Postgres if it's partitioned, but it changes the cost of every operation you perform on the cluster as a whole. Base backups get slower, your restore time objective gets worse, major version upgrades get harder to schedule, and all of that is being paid on behalf of data you read a handful of times a year, so the tax lands on the application rather than on the audit log that incurred it.

The join requirement is weaker than it looks. If an investigation needs to join audit events to user records to find out who did something, that implies the audit rows store a user id and little else, which is a correctness problem independent of where you put them. Joining a three-year-old event to the current users table tells you the actor's name, role and team as of today, not as of the event. An audit record should be a self-describing snapshot: actor identity, display name, role, tenant, all captured at write time. Once you denormalise that way, most investigations stop needing the join, and the ones that remain are usually "show me everything this person did", which is a filter on a denormalised column.

Concretely, then: same database, separate schema, monthly partitions, and a dedicated role holding INSERT and SELECT and nothing else. Keep a hot window attached, ninety days or a year depending on how far back support actually reaches, which is a couple hundred million rows at most and where joins against user records work normally on the rare occasion you want one. Detach older partitions on a schedule and write them to object storage as Parquet, where they cost almost nothing and stay queryable with Athena or DuckDB when compliance asks. You get joins where joins are useful and the seven-year tail stays out of your backup path.

One caveat on "append-only", since the phrase is carrying a lot of weight in this debate. A Postgres table whose writing role only has INSERT satisfies most auditors, but it isn't WORM: a superuser, or anyone with filesystem access, can rewrite history without leaving evidence. If your compliance regime genuinely requires immutability rather than an immutability policy, no Postgres configuration gets you there, and you want S3 Object Lock in compliance mode on the archived partitions. It's worth reading what the regime actually says before the team argues further, because the two readings point to different designs. Independently of that, a hash chain, each row carrying a hash of the previous row's contents, gives you tamper evidence for one column's cost and survives the move to object storage.

The case for shipping everything to a separate store from day one is real, but it's mostly about organisational boundaries rather than technical ones: it's much easier to argue the audit log can't be tampered with when the application holds no credentials that could tamper with it. If your auditors or your customers are actually asking that question, pay the cost and take the cross-store stitching as the price. If nobody is asking it, splitting by age gives you the same properties on the data that matters while investigations stay a single query.
