I'd ship it to a separate store — but "separate" should mean a second Postgres instance you own, not a new technology, and the write path should go through a transactional outbox rather than a dual write.

The volume argument is the one people usually fight about, and it's the least relevant. 2M events/day is ~25/sec average, a few hundred at peak. Postgres does not care. What matters is the accumulation: ~5 billion rows over seven years, call it 3–5 TB depending on payload size. That's not a throughput problem, it's a *restore* problem. Once audit data lives in your primary, every base backup, every PITR restore, every major-version upgrade, and every logical replication cutover drags several terabytes of write-once data that nobody needs online. Your application's recovery time becomes a function of your compliance retention window, which is a bad coupling to accept for the next seven years.

The join requirement is also weaker than it looks. If support is joining a three-year-old audit event against the current user record, they're getting a misleading answer — the email, role, and org membership have changed since. Audit events should capture the relevant subject attributes *at write time*: user id, email as it was, org, actor, IP. That's better audit practice independent of where you store it, and once you do it, the live join mostly evaporates. For the residual cases, an occasional support query can afford a postgres_fdw join or a two-step lookup; you're not optimizing an interactive path.

What you actually gain from separation is a credential boundary. If the audit log lives in the same database under the same connection, an application-level compromise or an ordinary migration script can rewrite history, and you have no technical basis for claiming otherwise to an auditor. A separate instance where the app role has INSERT and nothing else — no UPDATE, no DELETE — with retention enforced by a separate maintenance role, is a control you can actually point at. Add a hash chain over each row if your regime wants tamper-evidence.

Concretely: separate instance, declarative partitioning by month, and detach-and-archive old partitions to object storage as Parquet once they age past the online window (12–18 months covers nearly all investigations). Seven years of cold data on S3 with Athena or DuckDB over it costs a rounding error compared to seven years on provisioned SSD, and it satisfies retention just as well. Reach for a dedicated append-only or WORM product only if your compliance regime names one — otherwise you're buying operational unfamiliarity you don't need.

The real cost of separating is losing atomicity: the business transaction commits, the audit write fails, and you've silently lost an event. Don't solve that with a best-effort dual write. Insert the audit event into a small outbox table inside the same transaction as the application change, and have a relay ship it to the audit store and delete it on ack. You get the same all-or-nothing guarantee as same-database storage, at the price of a few seconds of lag and one background worker to operate.
