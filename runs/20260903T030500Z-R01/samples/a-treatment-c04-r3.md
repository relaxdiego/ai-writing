I'd keep it in Postgres — but partitioned, in its own schema with its own role, with an archival tier in object storage. Not a pure split.

The numbers don't justify a separate store on their own. 2M events/day is ~23 writes/sec average, maybe a few hundred at peak; that's nothing. The seven-year total is ~5B rows, which is real but is squarely in "monthly partitions" territory, not "Postgres can't do this" territory. Depending on row width you're looking at somewhere between 1.5 and 5 TB.

**The argument people underweight for colocating:** transactional consistency. If the audit row commits in the same transaction as the action it describes, you cannot have an action without its audit record. Ship to a separate store and you need an outbox table plus a relay — which means you're running Postgres-backed audit writes anyway, just with extra moving parts and a window where the two disagree.

**The join argument is weaker than it sounds,** and you should discount it in your own debate. Audit events should denormalize the actor's identity at write time — user ID, email, display name, role, as they were at the moment of the event. That's not a performance hack, it's correctness: an audit record that renders today's user row is lying about what happened in 2023. Once you do that, most support investigations need no join at all. The residual cases (find every event by users in org X) are rare enough to tolerate a slower path.

**The argument that actually favors separation is operational, not architectural.** 5B rows in your primary cluster inflate backup and restore times, lengthen major-version upgrades, and add vacuum and replication pressure to the database serving user traffic. That's the cost worth designing around — and partitioning plus detaching old partitions handles it. Keep 12–18 months hot; investigations almost never reach further back, and when they do, a slow query against archived data is acceptable.

**Immutability needs a real boundary regardless of where the table lives.** A separate store doesn't give you tamper-evidence for free, and same-cluster doesn't preclude it. Separate schema, a dedicated writer role with INSERT only, REVOKE UPDATE/DELETE from the application role, and — the part that matters for compliance — continuous export of sealed partitions to S3 with Object Lock in compliance mode. That's your actual seven-year retention artifact. The Postgres copy is the query interface; the WORM copy is the record.

The one thing that would flip me: if your compliance regime specifically requires the audit log to be outside the control of anyone who can administer the application database. That's an organizational requirement, not a technical one, and no amount of role separation inside the same cluster satisfies an auditor who reads it strictly. Worth confirming before the team spends another week arguing.
