I'd write to Postgres and tier out of it, rather than choosing between the two options as posed.

The strongest argument for the same database is atomicity at write time. An audit event records a business fact, and if it lands in a different store than the fact itself, you now have a dual-write problem: the transaction commits, the ship-to-audit-store call fails, and you have silently lost the record of a change that actually happened. For a compliance log that is the worst possible failure mode, because it fails open and quietly. Writing the audit row in the same transaction as the change it describes makes the log exactly as durable as the data, with no reconciliation machinery. If you do ship elsewhere, you need a transactional outbox to get the same guarantee, which means you are writing to Postgres first anyway and have merely added a hop.

The strongest argument for separation is not integrity but operations, and it only bites at the tail. Seven years at 2 million events a day is roughly five billion rows; at a kilobyte each with indexes you are looking at multiple terabytes. Your write rate is trivial, about 25 per second averaged, so ingestion is not the concern. The concern is that this mass becomes the dominant cost of every operational task on your application database: backup and restore windows, major version upgrades, replica rebuild time, storage on provisioned IOPS you are paying for so that transactional queries stay fast. Restoring the application database during an incident should not mean waiting on four terabytes of history nobody is querying.

Both concerns are satisfied by keeping the audit table in Postgres but treating age as the boundary. Partition by month, keep a hot window online, say ninety days or a year depending on how far back support actually reaches, and move sealed older partitions to object storage in a columnar format with object lock or equivalent immutability. Detaching and archiving a partition is cheap and does not touch the live table. Older data stays queryable through whatever external-table mechanism your stack offers, at seconds-to-minutes latency rather than milliseconds, which is the right trade for a query that runs a few times a month.

On the join requirement, I would push back on it slightly, because it argues for less coupling than it appears to. Joining audit events to current user records is semantically wrong for an audit log: the user's email, role and team have changed since the event, and the investigation almost always wants to know what was true at the time. Denormalise the actor's identifying attributes into the event at write time and the join largely disappears. What remains is "show me everything actor X did", which needs an index on actor_id, not a foreign key. Keep the foreign key relationship out of it entirely, since audit rows must survive user deletion and a cascade here would be a compliance incident.

On tamper resistance, the same-database choice is weaker than a dedicated append-only store but not as weak as it is usually made out to be, and you can close most of the gap cheaply. Put the audit table in its own schema, have the application write through a role with INSERT only, revoke UPDATE and DELETE, and add a hash chain over each row so that any retroactive edit breaks verification from that point forward. A determined DBA with superuser can still rewrite history, but they can't do it invisibly, and tamper evidence is what auditors actually ask for. Periodically publishing chain checkpoints somewhere outside the database gives you the rest.

Where I would change my answer: if your compliance regime specifically names an independently administered store, or if separation of duties means the people who operate the application database must not be able to touch audit data at all, then the requirement is organisational rather than technical and the tiering scheme won't satisfy it. Write to the archive store directly through an outbox in that case, and accept the reconciliation work as the cost of the mandate. Absent that, the split in your team is probably between people weighing write correctness and people weighing long-term operational load, and those two concerns live at different ends of the data's lifetime rather than in conflict.
