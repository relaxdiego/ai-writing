I'd put it in Postgres, but not in the same database as your application data, and I'd tier it so the live cluster never holds all seven years.

Start with the mass you're actually committing to. Two million events a day for seven years is about 5.1 billion rows; at a realistic 500 bytes to 1 KB per event once you include the payload, that's three to five terabytes of heap before indexes. The write rate itself is nothing, roughly 23 inserts a second on average and maybe a few hundred at peak, which Postgres will absorb without noticing. So the argument against colocation isn't throughput, it's that everything operational on that cluster starts being sized by the audit log rather than by the application. Your base backups, your restore time, your replica rebuild, your major-version upgrade window, your vacuum and index maintenance all inflate by an order of magnitude, and when you need point-in-time recovery because someone shipped a bad migration against a user table, you are restoring four terabytes of immutable audit history to fix it. Same engine, separate cluster, costs you very little and decouples those lifecycles cleanly.

The join requirement matters less than it looks, because a correct audit record shouldn't be joining to live user rows anyway. If support asks what happened in March 2023, the answer should reflect the actor's email, role and organisation as they stood in March 2023, not as they stand today. Denormalise the actor and target attributes into the event at write time and most of the join disappears, replaced by something more defensible under audit. What's left is the case where an investigator has only a current user ID and needs to find their history, and that's satisfied by carrying a stable immutable user ID on every event plus either a small replicated copy of the user dimension in the audit database or a `postgres_fdw` link. These are human-timescale queries run a few times a week, so a two-step lookup or a foreign-table join at a few hundred milliseconds is entirely acceptable.

For the layout, partition by month on the event timestamp and keep something like twelve to eighteen months hot, which is roughly 700 million rows and a few hundred gigabytes. Append-ordered time data is the ideal case for a BRIN index on the timestamp, which costs almost nothing, and you add selective btrees on actor ID and target ID because investigators arrive with an identity more often than a time window. Older partitions get detached and exported to Parquet in object storage, where they stay queryable ad hoc through DuckDB or Athena on the rare occasion a five-year-old question comes up.

On append-only, be precise with your compliance people about what they're asking for, because Postgres can give you insert-only but not true immutability. Revoking UPDATE and DELETE from the writing role, inserting through a security-definer function, and adding a trigger that raises on modification will stop the application and ordinary operators, and a hash chain over consecutive rows gives you tamper evidence. None of it constrains a superuser or anyone with filesystem access. If the requirement is genuine WORM, only object storage with an object-lock retention policy satisfies it, and the archival tier gives you that for the bulk of the history while the hot window stays a normal database you can actually query.

The thing I'd avoid is adopting a dedicated audit or event store as the primary home. It buys you immutability you can approximate cheaply, and charges you a second system to operate, a second backup story, and the loss of ordinary SQL at exactly the moment someone is trying to answer a compliance question under time pressure.
