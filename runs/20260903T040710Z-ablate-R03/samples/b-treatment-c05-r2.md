I did not find the cause. Here is what the evidence does and does not support, so you can judge how much weight to put on the leading suspicion.

The short runs are not truncated, they are short from the start. Runtime tracks row count almost exactly: the three low nights (23rd, 26th, 29th August) took about seven minutes for roughly 23,000 rows, while the normal nights took about fifteen for roughly 48,000. Every run recorded status `ok`, and the writes are wrapped in a single transaction, so a crash partway through the write loop would have rolled back rather than committed half. The job is being handed about half the input, or fetching about half of it, and then doing its work correctly on that.

That points at the window. `scheduler.py` computes `window_start = last_success_at or (now - timedelta(hours=24))`, so the window is only 24 hours when `last_success_at` is absent; otherwise it is whatever that column happens to hold. A window of roughly twelve hours would produce roughly half the rows and roughly half the runtime, which is what the table shows. I did not verify this, and it is the gap that matters most: I never read the code that writes `last_success_at`, so I do not know whether it is set from the run's start time or its finish time, nor whether anything other than the nightly job updates it. My `job_runs` query filtered on `job='nightly_rollup'` and showed eight of thirty rows, so a second process or a manual re-run landing between nightlies would not have appeared.

Against that hypothesis is the cadence. The low nights fall three days apart, and neither a stale-timestamp bug nor an extra intervening run has any obvious reason to recur on a three-day cycle. I only looked at eight nights, so the period could be coincidence, but as it stands the mechanism I am proposing does not explain the regularity I observed.

Two other things I did not rule out. Commit 9d02e1e retries transient fetch errors, and a retry that returns a partial page while reporting success would produce exactly this signature; I did not read the retry logic or check when it shipped relative to the first low night. And the upstream event volume itself could genuinely halve on those nights, which would make the job innocent.

The cheapest way to separate these is to log `window_start` and `window_end` alongside `rows_written`, then compare the fetched row count against a direct count of source events in the same interval on the next low night. That distinguishes a short window from a short fetch from a genuinely quiet upstream, which is the fork the current data cannot resolve.
