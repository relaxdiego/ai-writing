Keep the audit log in the same Postgres database, partitioned by month, and tier partitions older than your active support window out to object storage. Don't run a second online datastore for this.

The deciding argument is the write path, not the read path. An audit record that exists only if a second system accepted it is a record you will eventually lose, because the application will commit a change and then fail to publish the event. Fixing that means a transactional outbox and a relay, so you end up writing to Postgres anyway and paying for the shipping machinery on top. Writing the audit row in the same transaction as the change it describes gives you the guarantee for free: if the change committed, the audit record committed. For a compliance log, that property is worth more than anything a separate store offers.

The volume does not argue against this. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second on average and maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for Postgres. Storage is the real number to watch: 5.1 billion rows over seven years, somewhere between 1.5 TB and 5 TB depending on how wide your event row is, before indexes. Measure your actual row before committing to a plan, since a JSONB payload with a couple of GIN indexes lands at the top of that range and a narrow row with a btree on `(user_id, created_at)` lands near the bottom. What that storage costs you is not query performance, since a partitioned table only scans the partitions a time-bounded query touches. It costs you backup duration, PITR archive size, and replica rebuild time, and those are the things that will actually hurt on a primary that also serves application traffic.

That is what the tiering is for. Keep whatever window support genuinely investigates in live partitions, probably 12 to 18 months, and confirm that number against your actual ticket history rather than guessing. Detach older partitions, write them to object storage as Parquet, and drop them from the database. You keep the join against user records where you need it and where user rows still exist, and the seven-year obligation is met by data you can produce on request rather than data you can query in 50 milliseconds. Give the audit tables their own role with no UPDATE or DELETE grant, point support queries at a read replica, and set an aggressive `statement_timeout` there so a careless scan can't drag the primary down.

The case for a separate store usually comes down to one of three things, and it's worth naming which one your team is actually arguing about:

- **Immutability.** A different database doesn't give you this. Anyone with write access to that store can still alter rows. Real tamper evidence comes from hash-chaining each record to its predecessor and periodically anchoring the chain digest somewhere with WORM semantics, such as S3 Object Lock in compliance mode. You can do that in Postgres today, and you'd have to do it in the separate store too.
- **Isolation.** Legitimate, and the tiering plan addresses it. If it isn't enough, a separate Postgres instance with the same schema costs far less than a different technology and preserves the option to restore a partition and join locally.
- **Cost.** Also legitimate at the tail end, which is exactly why the old partitions go to object storage rather than staying on provisioned SSD.

The one thing that would change my recommendation is a regulator or auditor who has specifically required that audit data live outside the systems it audits, on the theory that an operator with production database access shouldn't be able to touch their own trail. That's an organisational requirement rather than a technical one, and no amount of partitioning satisfies it. Find out whether anyone has actually imposed it before the team spends another week arguing about throughput.
