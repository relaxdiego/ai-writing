**Separate Postgres instance — same engine, different blast radius.** I'd push back on both poles of the split.

Against keeping it in the application database: throughput isn't the problem. Two million events a day is about 23 writes/second average, maybe a few hundred at peak, which Postgres won't notice. The problem is that seven years of retention is roughly 5.1 billion rows, and at a realistic 400–800 bytes of payload plus tuple overhead and a couple of indexes you're looking at 5–7 TB. Putting that in the application database means your production RTO is now governed by data that never serves a user request. Base backups, restore drills, replica rebuilds, and major-version upgrades all get slower in proportion to bytes you only read during investigations. That coupling is the real cost, and it shows up on the worst day you'll have.

Against shipping it somewhere new: your support investigation workflow is ad-hoc SQL written under time pressure by someone who needs an answer now. Kafka plus object storage plus a query engine turns a two-minute question into a pipeline, and it adds an operational surface your team doesn't already know how to page for. Unless something forces it, keep the query language you have.

What the "append-only store" camp actually wants is enforcement, and you can get most of it in Postgres. Give the application a role with `INSERT` only — no `UPDATE`, no `DELETE`, no `TRUNCATE` on the events table — and put schema-change rights in a separate credential the application never holds. Add a per-row hash chain (each row commits over its own content plus the previous row's digest) with a periodic verification job, so tampering that bypasses the grants is still detectable. Between the grant separation and the chain you can demonstrate immutability to an auditor without leaving the database.

On the join: I'd denormalize the actor and subject identity into the event at write time regardless of where the log lives, because joining to live user records is subtly wrong. An investigation into what happened eighteen months ago should show the email and role the actor had *then*, not what the row says today. Once the events carry their own snapshot, most investigations need no join at all. For the remainder, `postgres_fdw` back to the application database works fine at investigation-sized result sets, and a nightly-synced slim user dimension in the audit database covers anything that needs to be fast.

Partition by month from day one — 84 partitions, roughly 60M rows each, which keeps pruning effective and makes expiry a `DROP TABLE` rather than a seven-hour delete. I'd also plan to tier: export partitions older than eighteen months to compressed Parquet in object storage and drop them locally, with a documented and *tested* restore path. Most regimes accept retrieval within a few business days for older records, and it holds your hot footprint near 1 TB instead of 7.

The one thing that would change this answer is a regulator explicitly requiring WORM media or third-party attestation. If that's in scope, write to S3 with Object Lock in compliance mode as the system of record and keep a queryable index in Postgres pointing at it — but confirm the requirement is real before paying for it, because "append-only" in a policy document usually means enforced-and-verifiable, which the design above already satisfies.
