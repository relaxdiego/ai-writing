## Where I'd land

Keep the **write path** in Postgres, keep a **hot window** (~90 days) there as a partitioned append-only table, and age everything older out to compressed object storage with WORM retention. That's not a dodge of the question — it splits the two things your team is actually arguing about, which are durability of the write and economics of the tail. Those have different right answers.

**The write belongs in Postgres.** Your volume is ~23 events/sec average, maybe a few hundred at peak. That is nothing; throughput is not the constraint here and shouldn't drive the decision. What matters is that a compliance audit log must not drop events, and the only cheap way to guarantee that is to write the audit row in the *same transaction* as the business change it records. If the audit insert fails, the business operation fails. Any design that ships events to an external store at write time — Kafka, a managed audit product, an HTTP sink — turns this into a dual-write problem, and you'll spend the next two years chasing gaps that your auditor will eventually find. If you want the events elsewhere, use an outbox: write in-transaction, ship asynchronously, delete locally only after the remote store confirms.

**The seven-year tail does not belong in Postgres.** 2M/day × 7 years is roughly 5.1 billion rows. At a plausible 400–800 bytes per event with a jsonb payload, that's several TB raw and likely 8–15 TB with indexes — and you'd be paying for it on provisioned SSD, times your replica count, inside your application database's backup and restore path. That last part is the real cost. Your app DB's recovery time objective becomes dominated by data nobody needs during a recovery. Restoring 12 TB to get back a 200 GB application is a bad Tuesday. The same events as zstd-compressed Parquet in object storage will compress hard (audit events are extremely repetitive) and cost single-digit dollars a month, with S3 Object Lock or equivalent giving you a genuinely immutable store — which is a stronger compliance control than any append-only guarantee you can enforce from inside a database you also have admin on.

**The join requirement is weaker than it sounds, and partly an anti-requirement.** Denormalize the actor and subject attributes into the event at write time: `actor_id`, plus their email, display name, and role *as of the event*. You want this regardless. An audit record that resolves the actor by live foreign key doesn't tell you who did the thing — it tells you what that row looks like today. A rename, a role change, or a GDPR deletion silently rewrites your history. Once attributes are captured at write time, the "join" collapses into filtering by `actor_id`, and support investigations pull tens or hundreds of rows at human latency. For an investigation reaching past the hot window, load the relevant month's Parquet into a temp table (DuckDB, Athena, whatever) and join there.

## Concrete shape

1. `audit_events` in the app database, declaratively partitioned by month.
2. Append-only by grant, not by convention: application role gets `INSERT`/`SELECT` only, no `UPDATE`/`DELETE`, and a trigger that raises on either. If your auditor wants tamper-evidence, add a `prev_hash` chain.
3. Typed columns for what you query on — `occurred_at`, `actor_id`, `action`, `resource_type`, `resource_id`, `request_id`, `ip` — plus jsonb for the variable payload. BRIN on `occurred_at` (near-free on append-ordered data), btree on `(actor_id, occurred_at DESC)` and `(resource_type, resource_id, occurred_at DESC)`. Resist a GIN index over seven years of jsonb.
4. Monthly job: detach the oldest partition, export to Parquet, verify row counts and checksums, write to object storage under a seven-year lock, then drop.

The one number I'd actually measure before committing: what fraction of support investigations reach back more than 30, 60, and 90 days. That's the only input that should set the hot-window size, and you probably already have the query logs to answer it. If 99% are inside 30 days, 90 is already generous and you could go tighter.
