Ship it to a separate append-only store, and keep a bounded recent window in Postgres for the support case.

The number that decides this isn't 2 million a day. That's about 23 writes a second, which Postgres will not notice. It's 2 million × 365 × 7: roughly 5 billion rows, somewhere between two and five terabytes before indexes. Once that lives in the application database, everything operational about the application becomes governed by your compliance retention: backup windows, restore time after an incident, replica rebuild, major-version upgrades, freeze behaviour on a table nobody reads. You'd be paying for premium transactional storage, replicated, to hold seven-year-old rows that get read a handful of times a year.

The join requirement is the colocation side's strongest argument, and it's weaker than it looks, for two reasons. Support investigation is a human-latency task, so fetching audit rows from one store and resolving user IDs against the app database in a second query is perfectly acceptable. More importantly, joining audit events against live user records answers the wrong question: it tells you what the actor's email and roles are today, not what they were when the event happened. A defensible audit record carries the relevant identity attributes as they stood at write time, denormalised into the event. Once you do that, which you should do regardless of where the log lives, most of the join disappears.

The other thing a separate store buys you is genuine immutability. Postgres is not append-only. You can revoke UPDATE and DELETE, but anyone with superuser can still rewrite history, and that is an awkward answer to give an auditor. Object-lock storage or a purpose-built ledger gives you a claim you can actually evidence.

Concretely, I'd write events to Postgres much as you do now, into a partitioned table, but treat that table as a cache with 60 to 90 days of retention. That's around 150 million rows, entirely comfortable when partitioned, and it covers the overwhelming majority of investigations. Every event also goes to the durable store, which is the system of record: compressed columnar files in object storage under a lock policy, queried through Athena, BigQuery or DuckDB. Seven years of that is a few hundred gigabytes and single-digit dollars a month. Detach old Postgres partitions on a schedule, only after confirming the corresponding data has landed downstream.

The real cost here is a second system and a shipping path that has to be reliable. Make the object store the authority and reconcile Postgres against it, so a gap in Postgres is always recoverable and never the reverse.

Where I'd change my mind: if the events are small, the compliance obligation is nominal, and the team is thin, a single partitioned Postgres table with a detach-to-cold routine is defensible and considerably simpler. But at five billion rows against a seven-year regulatory requirement, the coupling costs more than the second system does.
