I'd write the audit log to the same Postgres instance, into a monthly-partitioned table, and move partitions older than your active support window into compressed object storage. If you force the choice as your team has framed it, colocate: the failure modes of a separate store are harder to recover from than the failure modes of a large Postgres table, and colocating leaves the door open to adding an archive tier later, whereas a separate store is painful to migrate back from.

Start with the volume, because I think it's doing less work in this debate than people assume. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes per second averaged, and perhaps a few hundred per second at your daily peak. Postgres will not notice that. What accumulates is the problem: roughly five billion rows over seven years, and at a few hundred bytes each with indexes on top, somewhere between four and six terabytes. The cost of that isn't the disk, it's what a table that size does to everything around it. Your backup and restore times start being governed by data that nobody needs during an incident, your logical replication and major-version upgrade windows stretch, and your storage bill on a managed tier grows against the most expensive storage you buy. Those are all real, and they're all reasons to get old audit data out of the primary, but none of them is a reason to keep it out in the first place.

The strongest argument against shipping directly to a separate store is atomicity. When the audit event and the change it records are written in the same transaction, the log cannot disagree with the database. Ship the event over the network instead and you immediately own two new bugs: events recorded for transactions that rolled back, and committed changes with no corresponding event because the shipper failed after commit. The standard fix is a transactional outbox, where you write the event to a Postgres table inside the transaction and a separate process forwards it. Notice what that means. The outbox is the audit table in Postgres, and you've arrived at the colocated design with an export step bolted to it.

The join requirement deserves interrogation rather than accommodation. Support investigations almost always look at one user over a bounded time range, which is a lookup rather than a relational join, and the fix is to denormalise the actor into the event at write time: user id, email, role, and whatever else identified them at the moment of the action. You want this independent of where the log lives, because an audit record that stores only a foreign key is a record that changes its meaning when the user is renamed, has their role revoked, or is deleted under a GDPR request. An audit event should be a self-contained snapshot of what was true when it was written. Once it is, the genuine cross-store join case shrinks to occasional analytical questions that tolerate a slow query.

What the separate store genuinely buys you is immutability you can demonstrate. In your application database, the roles that mutate application data can in principle mutate audit rows, and while you can revoke UPDATE and DELETE from the application role and add triggers, a superuser still can. Object storage with a WORM lock gives you a retention property enforced below your application, which is a materially easier thing to put in front of an auditor. It's also perhaps a tenth of the cost per byte once the data is written as compressed columnar files. Both of those benefits attach to the cold tier, which is exactly the part the hybrid moves out.

Mechanically: declarative range partitioning by month, a BRIN index on the timestamp, which is nearly free at this row count, and a btree on the actor identifier, which is what will actually cost you space. Keep something like twelve months live, which covers the overwhelming majority of support work and gives you the in-database join for free during that window. Detaching an old partition is a metadata operation, so a monthly job that detaches, writes the rows to Parquet under an object-lock policy, verifies the count and checksum, then drops the partition, keeps the primary at a steady size indefinitely. Compliance retrieval from the archive runs through DuckDB or Athena and takes seconds rather than milliseconds, which is the correct trade for data read a handful of times a year.

The thing that would change this answer is a regulator or contract that explicitly names write-once storage or third-party attestation for the system of record rather than for the archive. Under that constraint the external store has to be authoritative from the moment of write, and you should build the outbox and accept the operational cost, because no amount of REVOKE inside Postgres will satisfy an auditor who has been told to look for a specific storage guarantee.
