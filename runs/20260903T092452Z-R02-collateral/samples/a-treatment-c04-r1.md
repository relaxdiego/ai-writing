Keep it in the same Postgres, in its own schema, partitioned by month, with old partitions tiered out to object storage. The separate append-only store is solving a problem you don't have yet and creating two you do.

Start with the volume, because that's usually what drives people toward a separate store, and here it shouldn't. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second averaged out, call it a few hundred at peak. Postgres will not notice. The number that matters is the total: seven years is roughly 5.1 billion rows, which at a few hundred bytes each lands somewhere around 2 to 5 TB with indexes. That's a real operational weight, but it's weight you manage with declarative partitioning rather than with a second datastore. Monthly partitions give you 84 of them at about 60 million rows each, the planner prunes on the timestamp predicate that essentially every audit query carries, and dropping or detaching an expired partition is instant instead of a delete that runs for a day and leaves you vacuuming.

The join requirement is the first decisive thing. If audit events live somewhere else, every support investigation pays a tax: someone writes glue code, or exports a CSV, or maintains a stale replica of the user table in the other store so the join can happen there. You pay that cost on every investigation for the life of the system, and the people paying it are usually the ones with the least time. Keeping both in one database means a support engineer writes one query.

The second decisive thing is transactional integrity, and I'd weigh it above the join. When the audit row is written in the same transaction as the action it records, the audit log is correct by construction: no committed action lacks a record, no record exists for a rolled-back action. Ship events to a separate store and you need an outbox, a delivery worker, and an answer for what happens during the four hours the pipeline was down. "We believe we lost some audit events in March" is precisely the sentence a seven-year compliance retention exists to prevent.

It's also worth being clear that a separate store is not what makes a log append-only. Immutability comes from permissions and from tamper-evidence, not from which server the bytes sit on. In Postgres you revoke UPDATE and DELETE from the application role, write through a function owned by a role the app cannot assume, and hash-chain each row against its predecessor if you need to prove nothing was altered. A separate store your application can write to with full credentials is no more immutable than that, and often less.

Where the separate-store camp is right is backup and restore. A five-terabyte database has a slow restore and an expensive PITR window, and you don't want your recovery objective for application data set by audit history nobody has queried since 2021. That's what the tiering is for: keep 12 to 18 months live in Postgres, which is where support investigations actually land, and export detached partitions to compressed Parquet in object storage with S3 Object Lock and a documented, rehearsed restore path. Live data stays under a terabyte, compliance retention is satisfied, and the rare old-data question gets answered with DuckDB or Athena.

Three things would flip me:

- An auditor or regulator specifying WORM storage with legal hold as the system of record, in which case that store is mandated and Postgres holds a queryable copy for support.
- Write volume growing more than tenfold, or events carrying large payloads that push you well past ten terabytes.
- An application database already at its operational ceiling, where adding anything is the wrong move regardless of what it is.
