I'd keep it in Postgres, but not in the shape either side of your team is likely arguing for: same cluster, its own database or at minimum its own schema with its own roles, time-partitioned, and with anything older than roughly eighteen months detached and shipped to immutable object storage. That gives you most of the integrity and cost properties the append-only camp wants without surrendering the transactional and relational properties the other camp is defending.

Start with the numbers, because they're less alarming than they sound in one direction and more alarming in another. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes a second averaged, maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for Postgres; throughput is not your problem and nobody should be choosing an architecture around it. Seven years of it is roughly 5.1 billion rows, and at five hundred bytes to a kilobyte per event with a JSON payload you're looking at several terabytes before indexes. The cost of that isn't in writing it, it's in carrying it: backup windows, point-in-time-restore times measured in hours, autovacuum freeze pressure on a table that never gets updated but still has to be frozen against transaction ID wraparound, and major-version upgrades that now have to drag the whole thing along. Those costs land on your application database's operational envelope, which is why the instinct to move audit somewhere else is a sound one even though the reasoning usually offered for it is not.

The strongest argument for staying in Postgres is one that often goes unstated. If the audit row is written in the same transaction as the change it describes, you cannot get a business change without its audit record, and you cannot get an audit record for a change that rolled back. Ship events to a separate store over the network and you have re-created an exactly-once delivery problem, which in practice means an outbox table in Postgres anyway plus a relay process, plus monitoring for relay lag, plus a reconciliation job for when the relay silently falls behind during an incident. For a log whose entire value is that a regulator or an investigator can trust it, an eventually-consistent pipeline with a queue in the middle is a worse guarantee than a foreign key.

The join requirement is weaker than it looks, and worth interrogating before it drives the decision. A support investigation that joins a five-year-old audit event to the current user record is arguably producing a wrong answer, because the user's email, role and permissions have changed since the event happened. Audit events should carry a snapshot of the actor's identity at write time: id, email, role, tenant, whatever the investigation needs. Once you denormalise that way, the thing you actually need is not a join but an indexed lookup on actor_id, which works identically whether the data lives beside your users table or in Parquet files on S3. Keeping the live join available for recent data is still convenient, and that's what the hot partitions are for.

On tamper-evidence, you can get most of what a dedicated append-only store sells you. Revoke UPDATE and DELETE from every role including the application's, so the only grant on the table is INSERT. Hash-chain the rows, each one covering the previous row's hash, so any excision or edit is detectable rather than merely discouraged. Ship archived partitions to object storage with a WORM policy and a retention lock that your own operators cannot clear. What you cannot get is custody by a third party, and if your compliance regime specifically demands that the audit log sit outside the control of the people who run the application, then the decision is already made for you and no amount of Postgres tuning changes it.

So my honest read is that this is a partitioning and tiering problem that has been framed as a storage-engine problem. I'd flip to a genuinely separate store in two cases: if a regulator requires custody separation, or if your audit consumers turn out to be analytics-shaped, scanning hundreds of millions of rows to answer aggregate questions rather than pulling up one user's history. Neither is described in what you've laid out, and the seven-year retention that makes the volume scary is cold compliance data that almost never gets read, which is exactly what the archival tier is for.
