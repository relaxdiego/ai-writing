I'd keep audit events in the same Postgres cluster, in their own schema, partitioned by month with insert-only permissions — and then age old partitions out to cheap object storage after the investigation window closes. The split in your team is real, but I think the choice is framed on the wrong axis: the question isn't *which system*, it's *which time horizon*. Your retention requirement and your query requirement have wildly different shapes, and once you separate them the argument mostly dissolves.

Start with the volume, because it's usually where this debate goes wrong. Two million events a day is about 23 writes per second averaged out, maybe a few hundred at peak. As a write load against Postgres that's close to noise, and anyone arguing for separation on throughput grounds is solving a problem you don't have. The real cost is accumulated bulk: 730 million rows a year, 5.1 billion over seven years, and somewhere between two and five terabytes depending on how fat your event payloads are. That bulk does hurt, but it hurts in one specific place — your backup and restore path. If the application database is 200 GB and the audit table is 4 TB, your recovery time objective for the *application* is now hostage to data nobody is going to query during an outage. Partitioning plus tiering fixes that directly, without standing up and operating a second storage system.

The join requirement pulls hard toward colocation, and it's the strongest thing the "keep it together" camp has. Cross-store joins mean either pulling audit rows into the app and hydrating them by hand, or maintaining a replicated copy of user records in the audit system — both of which are real, permanent engineering cost paid on every investigation. But there's a trap hiding inside that requirement that you should fix regardless of which way you go. Audit events must be self-contained. If an investigator reads a two-year-old event and your query joins to `users` to render the actor's email, then a user changing their email, or being deleted under a GDPR request, silently rewrites what your audit trail appears to say about the past. Denormalize actor identity, target identity, and any other human-readable context into the event row at write time. The join to `users` then becomes a convenience for "what else do we know about this person today," not a correctness dependency — which also means colocation is a strong preference rather than a hard constraint.

That leaves the case for a separate append-only store, and I want to be precise about what it actually buys, because "separate" and "append-only" get conflated. A different database is not immutable by virtue of being different. Immutability comes from permissions and from cryptographic chaining, and you can have both in Postgres: grant the application role `INSERT` only, revoke `UPDATE` and `DELETE`, and carry a hash-chain column where each row commits to its predecessor so any retroactive edit breaks verification. The genuine thing a separate store gives you that this doesn't is a distinct failure domain — an attacker who compromises your application database credentials, or a well-meaning engineer with a bad migration, can't touch the audit trail. If that's in your threat model, the cheap version is to periodically anchor the hash chain somewhere the app database can't reach: publish the chain head to an append-only bucket or a signed offsite log every hour. You get tamper *evidence* for a few lines of code instead of tamper *prevention* for a whole second system.

Concretely, what I'd build: monthly partitions on the audit table; the last 90 days live and joinable, which is roughly 180 million rows and entirely comfortable; an insert-only role for writes and a separate read-only role for the support tooling; hash chaining with an hourly offsite anchor; and a detach-and-export job that converts partitions past the hot window into Parquet in object storage, queryable with DuckDB or Athena when an investigation genuinely reaches back years. Support looks at last-week data constantly and five-year-old data almost never, so the expensive capability — joinable, indexed, low-latency — only has to exist for the small recent slice.

Two things would change my answer. If you're subject to a regime that mandates WORM media specifically — SEC 17a-4 is the usual one — then a compliant store isn't an architecture choice and you should stop debating it. And if you're on a managed Postgres where storage is priced painfully and you can't attach cheap volumes or tier partitions out, the economics of keeping terabytes in the primary flip, and I'd ship to object storage much earlier, with a 30-day hot window instead of 90.
