I'd separate the storage without separating the technology: run the audit log in its own Postgres database, ideally its own instance, rather than as another set of tables alongside application data. That keeps SQL, your existing operational knowledge, and the ability to join, while cutting the couplings that make people nervous about the single-database option.

The volume argument is mostly a red herring in both directions. Two million events a day is about twenty-three writes per second averaged out, maybe a few hundred at peak, which is nothing for Postgres and no reason to reach for a specialised store. The pressure comes from retention rather than throughput. Seven years is roughly five billion rows, and depending on payload size that is several terabytes before indexes. If that sits in your application database it dominates your backups, your restore time, your logical dumps, and your major-version upgrades, which means the recovery time objective for the data your business actually runs on gets tied to hauling around log data nobody is querying during an outage. Separation buys you the freedom to back the two up on different schedules with different retention and different urgency.

The append-only camp is usually reaching for something real, but it is worth naming precisely, because it is not immutability in the abstract. You can get append-only semantics inside Postgres by granting a writer role INSERT only, revoking UPDATE and DELETE, and adding a trigger that raises on modification; if you need tamper evidence rather than tamper resistance, hash-chain each row against its predecessor and periodically sign the head. What none of that survives is a superuser on the same instance. If your compliance posture requires that the people who administer the application database cannot rewrite audit history, you need separation of duties enforced by separate credentials on separate infrastructure, and a separate schema in the same cluster will not give you that. Decide whether your auditors care about this, because it is the one argument that genuinely forces the split.

On the join requirement, I'd push back on the premise a little. Joining a three-year-old audit event against the live users table tells you what the actor is called today, not what they were called or what permissions they held at the time, and for support investigations that difference is occasionally the whole question. Denormalise the actor's identifying attributes into the event at write time: user id, email as it stood, role, tenant, whatever the investigation needs. That is better auditing regardless of where you store it, and it dissolves most of the join requirement as a side effect. For the residual cases, postgres_fdw across the two databases handles ad-hoc support queries perfectly well at the volumes a human investigation generates.

Whichever way you go, partition the audit table by time from day one, monthly giving you eighty-four partitions across the retention window. Keep twelve to eighteen months hot and roll older partitions out to Parquet in object storage with a documented retrieval path; compliance regimes almost always accept hours-to-retrieve for multi-year-old records, though confirm yours does before you build around it.

Two things would change my recommendation. If your regulator names immutable media specifically, in the SEC 17a-4 sense, then the system of record has to be object storage with object-lock and Postgres becomes an index over it. Conversely, if you have no operational capacity for a second instance, a single database with partitioning, a restricted writer role, and a written plan to split later is defensible, because partitioning is the decision that is painful to retrofit at five billion rows and the split is not.
