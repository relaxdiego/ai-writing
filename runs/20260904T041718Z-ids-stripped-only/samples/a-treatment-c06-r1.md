Your legal read is the stronger one: under GDPR a deletion request starts a clock on erasure, and a 30-day hold is defensible only when you can point to a specific justification like an active fraud investigation or a security hold. We don't have one, so the grace period was me optimizing for support tickets, which is not a lawful basis.

Two things genuinely cannot vanish at the moment of the click, and they're worth naming in the confirmation copy rather than treating as reasons to delay. Encrypted backups age out on their own rotation, so the standard and honest position is that the data is removed from production immediately, is never restored into production, and disappears from backups as they expire. Separately, invoices and payment records usually carry a statutory retention period of their own, so those rows stay with the personal identifiers stripped down to what the tax authority requires. Everything else goes synchronously.

For the implementation I'll wire the button to a confirmation step that requires re-authentication rather than a plain "are you sure", since immediate and irreversible means a hijacked session or a shared laptop can destroy an account with one click and no recovery path. On confirm: hard delete of the user row with cascades across their personal data, invalidation of every session and API token, and a deletion receipt sent to the address captured just before the row goes. Where a row is load-bearing for other users, such as a message in a shared thread or an actor id in the audit log, I'll anonymize to a tombstone id instead of deleting, because deleting there corrupts other people's records rather than protecting the departing user's.

The real cost you're accepting is that support can no longer undo a regretted deletion, and some fraction of users will ask. The answer becomes "we can't, by design", which is a defensible thing to say and a better product story than the one where you hold their data for a month after they asked you not to.
